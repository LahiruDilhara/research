\section{Chapter Overview}
\label{sec:chapter_overview}
Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) has developed rapidly over the past few decades. Researchers and developers continue to look for natural, simple, and low-cost ways for people to interact with computers. Traditional physical input devices, such as mechanical desktop keyboards, membrane keypads, and laptop touchpads, are still the most common tools for typing text and entering commands. Even though physical keyboards are widely used, their fixed plastic shapes, physical buttons, high manufacturing costs, and inability to change their key arrangements create practical challenges. For example, standard physical keyboards cannot easily adapt to custom user needs, assistive typing for disabled users, or sterile hospital environments \cite{ReviewVirtualKeyboard2020, Lee2022Virtual}.

To address these limitations, this research project presents a customizable, paper-based virtual keyboard system. The system works using a single ordinary monocular RGB webcam, AprilTag visual marker tracking, and a lightweight PyTorch deep learning model. The user can design any custom key layout using a desktop software application and print it on a normal sheet of plain paper with standard desktop printers. When the paper is placed on a flat table, a normal webcam observes the surface. Google MediaPipe Hand Landmarker tracks hand joint movements in 2D and 3D space. The joint coordinates are scale-normalized relative to hand size so the system works reliably across different cameras and distances. A lightweight Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural network runs on a standard commodity computer processor (CPU) in real time to detect when a finger presses the paper surface. When a touch is detected, planar homography transforms the camera pixel coordinates into the printed layout coordinate system to trigger the corresponding key press or system command. Crucially, the same physical printed sheet can be reused for multiple different software profiles (such as regular typing, developer shortcuts, sound editing, or terminal commands) without needing to reprint the paper.

\section{Problem Background}
\label{sec:problem_background}
Physical computer keyboards have been the primary method for entering text and commands for more than fifty years. While physical keyboards provide clear tactile feedback that helps trained typists type quickly, their fixed physical structure brings several practical drawbacks \cite{ReviewVirtualKeyboard2020}. A standard physical keyboard is rigid. Its key sizes, shapes, and button positions cannot be modified after manufacturing. As a result, users cannot easily adapt a physical keyboard for non-standard language character sets, single-handed typing, specialized application shortcuts, or assistive setups for individuals with hand tremors or motor disabilities.

To overcome the rigid nature of physical hardware, researchers in computer vision and human-computer interaction have studied projection-based and camera-based virtual keyboards \cite{Zhang2001Visual, Srivastava2012RealTime, Khare2019QWERTY, Ji2018Local}. Early virtual keyboard systems often used optical laser projectors paired with dedicated infrared receivers or Time-of-Flight (ToF) depth cameras \cite{Lee2019Virtual, Kudale2016RealTime}. Depth sensors measure the physical distance between the camera and the user's hand along the Z-axis, which makes it easier to tell when a finger touches a surface. However, depth cameras and laser projection units are expensive, consume extra power, and are not built into ordinary laptops or low-cost computers. Other early systems attempted to detect surface touches using a single standard RGB camera by observing fingertip shadow movements \cite{Thomas2013Camera, Posner2012Single}. In practice, shadow-based touch detection is very unstable. Changes in room lighting, shadows from moving hands, dynamic lighting angles, and different table colors cause frequent false key presses, making shadow-based typing unreliable for daily use \cite{Yue2014Blind}.

In recent years, open-source deep learning frameworks such as Google MediaPipe Hand Landmarker have made it possible to track 21 skeletal hand landmarks in real time from regular RGB video streams \cite{Zhang2020MediaPipe, Sreenath2021Monocular, GilMartin2023Hand, Ivacko2024Edge, Andriyanov2025Improving}. Because MediaPipe learns the anatomical structure of the human hand, it tracks joint positions across different skin tones and lighting conditions much better than older skin-color segmentation methods \cite{SanchezBrizuela2023Lightweight}. Even with reliable hand tracking, detecting the exact moment a finger touches a flat sheet of paper from a single standard camera remains a difficult challenge \cite{Li2021CNN, Lee2022Virtual}. A standard 2D camera does not provide depth values. Differences in hand sizes, changing distances between the hand and the camera, and camera viewing angles mean that simple position thresholds cannot reliably distinguish between a finger hovering slightly above the paper and an actual surface press \cite{Maman2023TypeNet, Yoo2026WordLevel}.

Furthermore, many existing virtual typing research projects use fixed, hard-coded QWERTY layouts without giving users tools to design custom interfaces. If the paper sheet moves slightly or the camera tilts, fixed pixel coordinate mappings fail, causing key clicks to land in the wrong places \cite{Wang2016AprilTag2, Kallwies2020Determining, Shabalina2018ARTag, Kalaitzakis2021Fiducial, Pirchheim2011Homography, Zhang2022DynaKey}. Therefore, there is a clear need for an integrated system that combines custom layout design, printable paper with visual fiducial markers, real-time planar homography tracking, scale-normalized hand joint features without temporal delay, and lightweight deep learning touch detection that runs on standard computer processors.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem_statement}
Existing virtual keyboard approaches face a significant practical trade-off. They either depend on expensive specialized hardware (such as 3D depth cameras, Time-of-Flight sensors, infrared receivers, stereo camera pairs, or laser projection units) or require high-end graphics processing units (GPUs) processing high-resolution video streams. Because of these constraints, ordinary users with standard laptops and low-cost USB webcams cannot easily use a real-time, customizable virtual keyboard on plain paper.

\subsection{General Problem}
\label{subsec:general_problem}
At a broad level, traditional physical keyboards and dedicated input peripherals are expensive to replace, can be damaged by spilled liquids and dust, and present hygiene concerns in shared public computers or clinical environments \cite{ReviewVirtualKeyboard2020}. Although vision-based virtual keyboards offer a flexible, low-cost alternative, current solutions often rely on specialized depth sensors or active projection equipment that regular users do not own \cite{Lee2019Virtual, Pilkov2024Markerless}. Meanwhile, many deep learning vision models require powerful GPU hardware and high-resolution camera feeds to run at acceptable speeds. This reliance on expensive hardware prevents paper-based virtual keyboards from being widely adopted in everyday home, school, and low-resource settings.

\subsection{Specific Problem}
\label{subsec:specific_problem}
Within the field of monocular computer vision and human-computer interaction, building an accurate and flexible virtual keyboard that works on plain printed paper using only a single regular RGB webcam on a standard CPU involves five specific technical problems:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Detecting Surface Touch Without Depth Hardware:} A single standard RGB camera does not provide direct depth information along the Z-axis. Telling whether a fingertip is touching a sheet of paper or hovering a few millimeters above it is difficult using simple position limits or fragile shadow analysis \cite{Thomas2013Camera, Srivastava2012RealTime}, often leading to false key presses.
    \item \textbf{High Computational Demand on Commodity Hardware:} Many modern deep learning models need dedicated GPUs to achieve real-time speeds. Running hand tracking and deep learning models together on a standard commodity CPU often causes high processor loads, latency delays, and dropped video frames if the pipeline is not carefully optimized.
    \item \textbf{Sensitivity to Camera Resolution and Distance:} Skeletal hand landmark detectors output pixel coordinates that change when the user moves closer to or further from the camera, or when camera resolution changes \cite{Zhang2020MediaPipe, Kumar2026Hybrid}. Without proper scale normalization, models trained on one camera setup perform poorly on low-resolution cameras (such as 360p or 480p) \cite{Simao2016Unsupervised}.
    \item \textbf{Perspective Distortion and Paper Movement:} When a paper layout is placed on a table, camera tilt and small paper movements change the positions of the keys in the camera view \cite{Wang2016AprilTag2, Oscadal2020Improved}. Without continuous planar homography tracking, the software maps touches to incorrect keys \cite{Kallwies2020Determining, Kalaitzakis2021Fiducial}.
    \item \textbf{Inflexibility and Inability to Reuse the Same Layout:} Most physical keyboards and research virtual keyboards use fixed QWERTY layouts with fixed key actions \cite{ReviewVirtualKeyboard2020}. They lack software tools that let users create custom layouts and link the same printed paper sheet to different action sets (such as regular typing, gaming controls, audio editing macros, or terminal commands) without reprinting the page.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Research Gap:} A clear research gap exists in developing a complete monocular vision framework that works on \emph{standard plain printed paper} using \emph{only a single regular RGB camera} (without requiring depth sensors, ToF cameras, stereo setups, laser projectors, or high-end optics), runs in \emph{real time on standard commodity CPUs without requiring a dedicated GPU}, supports \emph{custom user-designed layouts and dynamic action profile switching on the same printed sheet}, and maintains reliable touch accuracy across \emph{regular and low camera resolutions} using AprilTag homography tracking \cite{Wang2016AprilTag2, Kallwies2020Determining}, unitless hand-length scale normalization \cite{Zhang2020MediaPipe}, and a lightweight PyTorch LSTM touch classifier \cite{Hochreiter1997LSTM, Gammulle2021TMMF}.

\section{Research Question}
\label{sec:research_question}
To address this research gap, the project is guided by the following primary research question:

\begin{quote}
\textit{How can a monocular computer vision framework using strictly a single regular RGB camera and plain printed paper enable accurate, real-time, and flexible virtual keyboard interaction on flat surfaces (allowing users to design custom layouts and link different action combinations to the same physical printed sheet), executing entirely on standard commodity CPUs without requiring specialized hardware or dedicated GPUs, across regular and low camera resolutions?}
\end{quote}

The investigation addresses the following specific sub-questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item How can hand joint coordinates be scale-normalized to make feature extraction independent of camera resolution and distance while preserving finger touch deceleration dynamics?
    \item What temporal sliding window length, step size, and recurrent model architecture allow a lightweight LSTM network to detect touches accurately while running at a 12~FPS rate on a standard commodity CPU?
    \item How reliably can AprilTag planar homography correct for camera perspective tilt and paper shifts on a flat desk?
    \item How can physical paper layout geometry be separated from digital software actions so that the same printed layout can trigger different keystroke and command profiles?
\end{itemize}

\section{Research Motivation}
\label{sec:research_motivation}
This research is motivated by several practical and technical factors in human-computer interaction and accessible computing:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low Cost and Broad Accessibility:} By avoiding expensive depth sensors, laser projectors, or special hardware in favor of plain paper, an ordinary webcam, and a standard CPU, this system can be used on low-cost computers in schools, homes, and developing regions \cite{Khare2019QWERTY, Sreenath2021Monocular}.
    \item \textbf{Custom Layouts and Reusable Paper Sheets:} Users can design custom key layouts (such as number pads, gaming grids, or accessibility layouts) and print them on paper. The same physical printed sheet can be switched between different software modes (like standard typing, programming shortcuts, or audio production macros) without printing new sheets.
    \item \textbf{Hardware Independence Across Standard CPUs and Budget Cameras:} Ensuring that the software runs in real time on standard consumer CPUs without requiring a GPU, while working across regular and low camera resolutions (such as 360p, 480p, and 720p), allows the system to run on older laptops and budget computers.
    \item \textbf{Inexpensive and Disposable Paper Interfaces:} In industrial workshops, chemistry labs, and classrooms, custom keypads can be printed on paper for very low cost and replaced easily if damaged or soiled.
    \item \textbf{Hygienic Input for Medical Environments:} In hospital wards and operating rooms, physical keyboards collect bacteria; paper keyboard sheets with printed AprilTag anchors can be laminated for easy sanitization or discarded after single use.
    \item \textbf{Real-Time Deep Learning on Standard CPUs:} Showing that a compact PyTorch LSTM network can detect subtle finger deceleration patterns during surface impact in real time on a CPU without a GPU provides an effective approach for lightweight vision systems \cite{Hochreiter1997LSTM, Zhang2020MediaPipe, GilMartin2023Hand, Gammulle2021TMMF}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Research Aim}
\label{sec:research_aim}
The primary aim of this research is to design, develop, and evaluate a customizable, paper-based virtual keyboard system. The system allows users to create custom layouts on plain paper and dynamically connect the same printed sheet to multiple distinct action profiles (keystrokes, keyboard shortcuts, and executable shell commands). It operates using only a single regular monocular RGB webcam without specialized hardware (such as depth cameras, Time-of-Flight sensors, or stereo rigs), tracks plain paper layouts with AprilTag fiducial anchors, and runs in real time on standard commodity CPUs without requiring a GPU across regular and low camera resolutions.

\section{Research Objectives}
\label{sec:research_text_objectives}
To accomplish the research aim, the work is divided into four research objectives:

\subsection{1.7.1 To Identify...}
\label{subsec:obj_identify}
To identify and review theoretical principles, planar homography algorithms, hand tracking models, and feature normalization methods in existing literature, establishing the baseline requirements for single RGB camera tracking, plain paper homography, decoupled action profile mapping, and real-time CPU execution without specialized hardware.

\subsection{1.7.2 To Analyze...}
\label{subsec:obj_analyze}
To analyze hand joint kinematic features, unitless hand-length scale normalization, direct feature propagation, and temporal sliding windows (5 frames with a 2-frame overlap at a standardized 12~FPS rate) to achieve camera resolution independence and real-time CPU performance without needing a GPU.

\subsection{1.7.3 To Design and Develop...}
\label{subsec:obj_design}
To design, implement, and integrate an end-to-end two-application software suite comprising:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A PySide6 desktop layout designer (Application 1) for interactive key layout creation, XML file generation, and printable PDF export with embedded AprilTag markers on standard plain paper.
    \item A runtime setup and command mapper GUI (Application 2, Component A) that allows users to select regular RGB webcam feeds, load layout XML files, map buttons to keystrokes or shell commands, and save or switch different action profiles for the same printed paper layout.
    \item An AprilTag visual tracking module to compute a real-time $3 \times 3$ Planar Homography matrix ($H$) from single camera video, correcting for perspective tilt and paper shifts across regular and low camera resolutions \cite{Wang2016AprilTag2, Kallwies2020Determining}.
    \item A MediaPipe Hand Landmarker tracking module paired with a hand-length scale normalizer that processes raw keypoints directly without temporal smoothing \cite{Zhang2020MediaPipe, SanchezBrizuela2023Lightweight}.
    \item A lightweight PyTorch LSTM touch detection model (under 1~MB file size) trained on 12~FPS 5-frame temporal windows to detect finger touch contact in real time on standard CPUs without a GPU \cite{Hochreiter1997LSTM, GilMartin2023Hand, Simao2016Unsupervised}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{1.7.4 To Evaluate...}
\label{subsec:obj_evaluate}
To evaluate the complete system in terms of touch classification accuracy (precision, recall, F1-score), homography coordinate mapping precision, multi-profile action dispatch reliability on the same printed layout, CPU real-time latency across different camera resolutions (360p, 480p, 720p, 1080p), typing speed (Words Per Minute), Character Error Rate (CER), and overall usability on plain paper setups.

\subsection{Detailed Operational Architecture Steps}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Layout Design and Export:} The user creates a custom key layout using the PySide6 desktop designer program. The software outputs two synchronized files: an XML file defining key regions, button values, action commands, and marker anchor locations; and a printable PDF sheet showing the layout grid with AprilTag markers placed along the borders.
    \item \textbf{Physical Paper Setup and Capture:} The user prints the PDF on standard paper and places it on a flat table. A normal USB webcam or laptop camera captures the live video feed at any standard resolution.
    \item \textbf{AprilTag Tracking and Homography Calculation:} The AprilTag tracking module detects the four corner fiducial markers on the paper and extracts their 2D pixel coordinates \cite{Wang2016AprilTag2, Kallwies2020Determining, Shabalina2018ARTag, Kalaitzakis2021Fiducial}. It computes a $3 \times 3$ Planar Homography matrix ($H$) to map camera pixel coordinates directly into the XML layout coordinate system \cite{Pirchheim2011Homography, Herdiansyah2024Implementation, GarridoJurado2026ArUco}.
    \item \textbf{Hand Tracking and Scale Normalization:} MediaPipe Hand Landmarker extracts 21 skeletal joint keypoints for detected hands \cite{Zhang2020MediaPipe, SanchezBrizuela2023Lightweight, Sreenath2021Monocular, Ivacko2024Edge, Andriyanov2025Improving}. The coordinates are normalized relative to hand length ($L_{\text{hand}}$, the distance between the wrist and the middle MCP joint) to make features invariant to hand size, camera distance, and image resolution. Raw scale-normalized features are passed directly to the model without temporal smoothing to keep contact deceleration signals crisp.
    \item \textbf{Temporal Windowing:} Normalized joint positions and derived velocities are gathered at a 12~FPS processing rate into sliding temporal windows of 5 frames with a 2-frame overlap (a step size of 3 frames) \cite{Simao2016Unsupervised, Gammulle2021TMMF}.
    \item \textbf{LSTM Touch Classification on CPU:} The custom PyTorch LSTM network evaluates each 5-frame window on the CPU to classify whether a finger touched the physical surface \cite{Hochreiter1997LSTM, GilMartin2023Hand, Doan2022Efficient, Nunez2018Convolutional, Lai2018CNN}.
    \item \textbf{Homography Coordinate Mapping and Key Execution:} When a touch event is confirmed, the active fingertip pixel location ($P_{\text{pixel}}$) is mapped using $H$ into the XML layout frame ($P_{\text{XML}} = H \cdot P_{\text{pixel}}$). The coordinate is matched with key boundaries to trigger the mapped keystroke or system shell command.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Resource Requirements}
\label{sec:resource_requirements}
The hardware and software specifications needed to build, run, and test the customizable paper-based virtual keyboard system are listed below.

\subsection{Hardware Requirements}
\label{subsec:hardware_req}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Monocular Camera:} Any ordinary USB webcam, low-end camera, or built-in laptop camera working at regular or low resolutions (such as 360p, 480p, 720p, or 1080p). High frame-rate or high-resolution cameras are not needed.
    \item \textbf{Host Computer:} A standard PC or laptop with an x86\_64 or ARM processor (such as an Intel Core i3/i5, AMD Ryzen, or basic equivalent, with at least 4~GB of RAM). The system runs in near real time on standard consumer CPUs without requiring high-end processors.
    \item \textbf{Graphics Card (Optional):} Live inference runs entirely on the CPU. A dedicated GPU is optional and not required for daily runtime execution.
    \item \textbf{Physical Materials:} A standard desktop printer with normal A4 or Letter paper, and a flat desk or table surface.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Software Requirements}
\label{subsec:software_req}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Operating System:} Linux (Ubuntu 22.04 LTS or 24.04 LTS), macOS, or Windows 11.
    \item \textbf{Programming Language and Tools:} Python 3.10 or higher, managed using the `uv` package manager.
    \item \textbf{GUI Framework:} PySide6 (Qt for Python) for building desktop layout design and setup interfaces.
    \item \textbf{Computer Vision Libraries:} OpenCV (`opencv-python`) and Pupil AprilTag (`apriltag` Python bindings).
    \item \textbf{Hand Pose Estimation:} Google MediaPipe Hand Landmarker (`mediapipe`).
    \item \textbf{Deep Learning Framework:} PyTorch (`torch`, `torchvision`) for LSTM model definition, training, and real-time CPU inference.
    \item \textbf{Document Tools:} TeX Live full suite (`pdflatex`, `latexmk`, `biber`) for thesis compilation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Elaboration of Scope Boundaries}
The research explicitly restricts video acquisition to a single regular monocular RGB camera stream and uses standard plain printed paper sheets. This design maintains low cost, portability, and zero marginal hardware cost, eliminating any need for specialized depth hardware \cite{Posner2012Single, Enkhbat2020HandKey, Sreenath2021Monocular}. Hand tracking and touch classification are specifically focused on single-handed active interaction (tracking one active hand at a time). Focusing on single-hand interaction minimizes CPU processing overhead, keeping latency low ($29.09\text{ ms}$), while avoiding physical hand occlusion of the four corner AprilTag fiducial anchors printed on standard A4 paper sheets. The software pipeline is engineered around a 12~FPS processing target to ensure near real-time execution on standard commodity consumer CPUs without demanding dedicated GPU hardware at runtime (though GPU acceleration is supported where available) \cite{Doan2022Efficient, Li2021CNN, Lee2022Virtual}. In addition, through unitless hand-length scale normalization, the system eliminates any dependency on high-resolution or studio-grade camera optics, operating robustly across regular and low resolutions (such as 360p, 480p, and 720p). Planar homography mapping is restricted to flat 2D paper sheets on desks or tables \cite{Wang2016AprilTag2, Kalaitzakis2021Fiducial}. Multi-finger touch classification is performed across all five digits of the active hand using scale-normalized skeletal keypoint features processed by a lightweight PyTorch LSTM model \cite{Hochreiter1997LSTM, Zhang2020MediaPipe, GilMartin2023Hand}, optimized for standard CPU execution. Other features, such as simultaneous two-handed typing or curved surface unwrapping, are designated as out-of-scope for this phase of the project.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:chapter_summary}
This introductory chapter established the problem background, problem statement, research questions, motivation, aim, objectives, solution architecture, resource requirements, and project scope for a customizable paper-based virtual keyboard system.

By replacing costly specialized hardware (such as depth sensors, ToF cameras, laser projectors, and high-end GPUs) with a single regular RGB camera, standard plain printed paper, AprilTag planar homography tracking \cite{Wang2016AprilTag2, Kallwies2020Determining}, unitless scale normalization \cite{Zhang2020MediaPipe}, and a lightweight PyTorch LSTM touch classifier running in real time on standard commodity CPUs at 12~FPS \cite{Hochreiter1997LSTM, GilMartin2023Hand}, the research addresses key accessibility and technical challenges in monocular vision input interfaces. The next chapter, Chapter 2 (Literature Review), provides a comprehensive critical review of existing virtual keyboard frameworks, hand pose estimation algorithms, planar homography techniques, and deep learning gesture classification models in current research.
