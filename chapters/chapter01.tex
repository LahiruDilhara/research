\section{Chapter Overview}
\label{sec:chapter_overview}
The domain of Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) has evolved a great deal over recent years. Developers and researchers have always sought natural, intuitive, and inexpensive ways for humans to interact with computers. Traditionally, physically tangible input devices like mechanical keyboards, membrane keypads, and laptop touchpads have been extensively used for text and command inputs. Though physical keyboards have been in widespread use, issues like fixed shape made out of rigid plastic material, tangible buttons, manufacturing costs, and inability to customize the keys present several problems. Physical keyboards, for instance, are difficult to customize to suit specific user requirements, assistive typing for disabled users, and also the sterile environment of hospitals \cite{ReviewVirtualKeyboard2020, Lee2022Virtual}.

In an effort to mitigate some of the limitations presented by the physical keyboards, we propose a customizable paper-based virtual keyboard system in this research project. This system uses one conventional monocular RGB camera, AprilTag markers, and a lightweight PyTorch-based deep learning algorithm. Any arbitrary key layout may be designed and printed with standard desktop printers using a desktop application. Once the paper with the layout is put on a flat surface, it is observed by a normal camera. The hand joint motions are tracked in 2D and 3D space by Google MediaPipe Hand Landmarker. Coordinates of the joints are normalized with respect to the hand scale to ensure accurate detection independent of camera distance and type. A lightweight Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural network runs in real time on a commodity computer CPU to detect finger touches on the paper plane. Once a finger touch is detected, planar homography converts pixel coordinates from camera frame to the paper layout coordinate frame to invoke a key press or a command. Significantly, the same physical paper layout may be used for different software profiles (like regular typing, developer shortcuts, audio editing, or terminal commands) without reprinting the paper.

\section{Problem Background}
\label{sec:problem_background}
The physical computer keyboard has been an extensively used form of text input for more than fifty years. Although a physical keyboard can give immediate feedback on its usage that allows a well-trained typist to type faster, there are several limitations imposed by the rigidity of the physical keyboard that make it difficult to fit specific cases \cite{ReviewVirtualKeyboard2020}. The physical keyboard is a physical device. Its keys cannot be reconfigured after it has been manufactured, thus preventing a user from customizing his or her keyboard to type nonstandard characters, to do one-handed typing, to set up shortcuts for specific applications, or to accommodate people with hand tremor disorders.

In order to cope with the inflexibility of the physical hardware components, researchers of computer vision and human-computer interaction research area have considered several techniques based on projection and camera-based virtual keyboards \cite{Zhang2001Visual, Srivastava2012RealTime, Khare2019QWERTY, Ji2018Local}. In older solutions of virtual keyboards optical laser projector was used in conjunction with special infrared receiver or Time-of-Flight (ToF) depth camera \cite{Lee2019Virtual, Kudale2016RealTime}. Depth sensors calculate distance from camera to the hand of the user along Z axis. This helps with simplification of touches recognition. However, ToF depth camera and laser projector are costly, consume more power and are not included in standard laptops and desktops. Several earlier studies have attempted to detect touches on the projection plane by means of ordinary RGB camera using fingertip shadow tracking \cite{Thomas2013Camera, Posner2012Single}. It turned out that shadow touch recognition is very vulnerable to room illumination changes, hand movement shadows, changing illumination angles and different table color \cite{Yue2014Blind}.

The recent developments in the field of deep learning have led to the availability of some of the open source solutions like Google MediaPipe Hand Landmarker that can track 21 landmarks on hands using simple RGB videos in real time \cite{Zhang2020MediaPipe, Sreenath2021Monocular, GilMartin2023Hand, Ivacko2024Edge, Andriyanov2025Improving}. As MediaPipe relies on machine learning and learns how the human hand looks like, it is much more precise in terms of tracking joints independently from the skin color and lighting conditions than skin color-based segmentation used in older software \cite{SanchezBrizuela2023Lightweight}. Despite the successful hand tracking, the detection of the exact moment of contact between the finger and a paper sheet using a single standard camera remains difficult \cite{Li2021CNN, Lee2022Virtual}. A standard 2D camera doesn't provide any depth information. Different sizes of hands, varying distance from the hand to the camera, and different camera angles don't allow one to determine the point of contact based on the position thresholds \cite{Maman2023TypeNet, Yoo2026WordLevel}.

Finally, most recent research works on virtual typing are confined to QWERTY keyboards coded in the hard code form, thus offering no option for defining the keyboard interface for the user. Any motion of the paper or changing the angle of the camera would imply that the pixel coordinates won’t allow proper mapping of the keys anymore, leading to the clicks being detected in a wrong place \cite{Wang2016AprilTag2, Kallwies2020Determining, Shabalina2018ARTag, Kalaitzakis2021Fiducial, Pirchheim2011Homography, Zhang2022DynaKey}. For this very reason, it can be stated clearly that there is an evident necessity for a system incorporating custom keyboard layouts, printable paper with fiducial markers, homography tracking in real time, normalized features of hands’ joints, and deep learning touch detection algorithm on computer hardware.


\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem_statement}
Virtual keyboard solutions at present suffer from a critical problem regarding practicality. Firstly, there is a demand for complex and expensive hardware components, such as 3D depth cameras, time-of-flight sensors, infrared detectors, dual cameras, or laser projectors. On the other hand, the use of high-performance graphic processing units (GPUs) is needed to process high-resolution video streams. Consequently, people using standard laptops and cheap USB webcams cannot work with a live and personalized virtual keyboard on a regular printed sheet of paper.

\subsection{General Problem}
\label{subsec:general_problem}
From an overall view, the first limitation of conventional keyboards and other physical devices is that their replacement cost is high, they are easily affected by the spilling of liquids and gathering of dust particles and, lastly, the matter of hygienic nature for use on public systems or clinical environments \cite{ReviewVirtualKeyboard2020}. Although vision-based virtual keyboards offer various advantages such as flexibility, ease of use, and low costs, the current methods depend on specialized depth sensing or projecting units which are not usually owned by users \cite{Lee2019Virtual, Pilkov2024Markerless}. Another limitation of many deep learning vision algorithms is that they need advanced hardware like high-resolution cameras and powerful GPUs to work in real-time.


\subsection{Specific Problem}
\label{subsec:specific_problem}
In relation to monocular computer vision and human-computer interaction, developing an accurate, real-time and flexible virtual keyboard on a paper document using an off-the-shelf monocular RGB camera on a regular CPU is a difficult task as there are various challenges that exist in this area of technology:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Limitation of using single finger and multi-finger tracking:} The conventional monocular camera method of keyboard finger tracking works with just one active finger at any point in time \cite{Thomas2013Camera, Ji2018Local, Srivastava2012RealTime}. Shadow-based methods suffer from shadow occlusion when two or more fingers are moved simultaneously \cite{Posner2012Single}. Consequently, conventional vision systems cannot enable natural typing using all five fingers.
    \item \textbf{Identification of surface contact without depth sensor and hover duration:} It is difficult to distinguish between surface contact and hover without the help of depth sensor or hover duration. A regular RGB camera does not provide any depth information on the Z-axis \cite{Thomas2013Camera, Posner2012Single}. It is tough to determine whether the tip of the finger touches the paper or it just hovers a few millimeters above the paper surface. In addition, the earlier versions of these systems would make the user hover his/her finger on the key for a set amount of time \cite{Khare2019QWERTY, Chen2024Gaze}.
    \item \textbf{Latency in computation on standard computers:} Modern deep learning vision architectures typically need powerful GPU computations to run at real-time speeds, thus having high latency in computation on standard computers. Simultaneous operation of hand tracking and deep sequence modeling on a standard CPU can result in CPU overload and latency \cite{Enkhbat2020HandKey, Ji2018Local}.
    \item \textbf{Sensitivity to lighting, shadows, and skin tones:} A shadow detection and threshold approach cannot work effectively where there is diffuse indoor lighting, multiple light sources, soft hand shadows, different skin tones, and complex backgrounds \cite{Thomas2013Camera, Yue2014Blind}.
    \item \textbf{Perspective distortion, camera angle, and motion of the paper:} When the paper keyboard is placed on a desk, due to the tilt of the camera and motion of the paper, keys start moving within the frame \cite{Wang2016AprilTag2, Oscadal2020Improved}. This leads to inaccurate touch detection without planar homography \cite{Kallwies2020Determining, Kalaitzakis2021Fiducial}.
    \item \textbf{Lack of flexibility in layout and multiplexing of actions:} In the past, standard keyboards and even virtual keyboards followed a fixed layout pattern like QWERTY and actions were hard-coded into them \cite{ReviewVirtualKeyboard2020}. There is no system available which allows users to create their own custom layout along with assigning different action sets to the same paper without the need of re-printing it again.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Research Gap:} It appears that there is an obvious research gap in the development of a monocular vision system based solely on standard printed paper along with a normal RGB camera, which can recognize individual touch actions of multiple fingers without artificial delays, run in real time without the need of a dedicated GPU, support customized layout designs and switching of dynamic action profiles using the same printed paper, and ensure accurate touch recognition for both high and low resolution camera images by means of AprilTag homography-based tracking \cite{Wang2016AprilTag2, Kallwies2020Determining}, normalized unitless hand-length scale \cite{Zhang2020MediaPipe}, and simple PyTorch LSTM-based finger touch classification \cite{Hochreiter1997LSTM, Gammulle2021TMMF}.

\section{Research Question}
\label{sec:research_question}
In order to fill the gap found in the literature, the main research question that will guide this study is the following:

\begin{quote}
\textit{How is it possible for a monocular computer vision system employing only a single RGB camera as well as regular printed paper to facilitate precise, instantaneous, and adaptable interactions with the virtual keyboard that works in any plane surface where users are able to create their own layout and bind multiple actions with one physical sheet of paper, all running in general purpose CPUs without any special hardware?}
\end{quote}

To answer the main research question systematically and comprehensively, the research explores the following five subsidiary questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Fiducial marker choice and tracking of layout under perspective:} Which visual fiducial marker scheme, e.g., AprilTag, ArUco, ARTag, or STag, would result in the highest planar homography precision and least jitter in the corners under the perspective rotation of the camera and low video resolution conditions, and how to track the paper layout with occlusion of markers?
    \item \textbf{Combination and subset of hand landmarks and kinematic features:} What is the optimal combination and subset of the 21 MediaPipe hand joint landmarks together with hand-length scale normalization and joint velocities that yield the highest discriminatory power for finger touch detection?
    \item \textbf{Sequence architecture optimization for deep learning:} What type of neural network architecture, such as 1D CNN, Attention/Transformer, ResNet, BiLSTM, or LSTM, gives us the best results in terms of touch detection accuracy and F1 score, but with the speed less than 3~ms at the same time?
    \item \textbf{Temporal Windowing and Real-Time CPU Synchronization:} For what time length, step, and sampling rate of the temporal sliding window can the multi-threaded vision processing pipeline reliably detect impact deceleration dynamics when operated at 12~FPS on commodity CPUs without a GPU?
    \item \textbf{Layout separation and action multiplexing:} What is the way to make the geometric structure of paper layout physically independent from software semantics in order for one paper printout to be capable of being associated with several actions, such as typing, shortcuts, and shell commands?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Motivation}
\label{sec:research_motivation}
There are many practical and technological reasons why this research can be helpful in human-computer interaction and accessible computing:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low Cost and Broad Accessibility:} Using no depth-sensing devices, laser projectors, or specialized hardware, but rather just plain paper, a common webcam, and a standard processor makes it possible to use the technology on inexpensive PCs in schools, at home, and in developing regions \cite{Khare2019QWERTY, Sreenath2021Monocular}.
    \item \textbf{Custom Layouts and Reusable Paper Sheets:} Users can design customized key configurations (such as number pads, gaming grids, or assistive configurations) and print them on paper. The same physical key configuration (printed sheet) can be reconfigured to work in different software modes (for instance, standard typing, programming shortcuts, or audio editing macros) without reprinting new sheets.
    \item \textbf{Hardware Independence Across Standard CPUs and Budget Cameras:} Making sure that the software works in real time on standard consumer CPUs, without requiring GPU usage and operating across various camera resolutions (such as 360p, 480p, and 720p), makes it possible to run the system on older laptops and budget computers.
    \item \textbf{Inexpensive and Disposable Paper Interfaces:} In industrial workshops, chemistry laboratories, and classrooms, custom keypads can be printed on paper at very low cost and replaced easily if damaged or soiled.
    \item \textbf{Hygienic Input for Medical Environments:} In hospital wards and operating rooms, physical keyboards collect bacteria; paper keyboard sheets with printed AprilTag markers can be laminated for easy sanitization or simply discarded after use.
    \item \textbf{Real-Time Deep Learning on Standard CPUs:} Showing that a compact PyTorch LSTM network detects finger deceleration patterns during surface contact in real time on a CPU without GPU usage provides an effective approach for lightweight vision systems \cite{Hochreiter1997LSTM, Zhang2020MediaPipe, GilMartin2023Hand, Gammulle2021TMMF}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Research Aim}
\label{sec:research_aim}
The main goal of this research is the design, development, and evaluation of a customizable, paper-based virtual keyboard system. This system allows users to create custom designs on plain paper and associate the same paper printout with multiple individual action profiles (keystrokes, keyboard shortcuts, and executable shell commands). It works solely with the help of a single monocular RGB webcam without specialized hardware (such as depth cameras, Time-of-Flight sensors, or stereo rigs) by tracking plain paper layouts with AprilTag fiducials and running in real time on commodity CPUs without a GPU even at low camera resolutions.

\section{Research Objectives}
\label{sec:research_text_objectives}
To accomplish the research aim, the work is divided into four research objectives:

\subsection{1.7.1 To Identify...}
\label{subsec:obj_identify}
To review and investigate theoretical concepts, planar homography techniques, hand tracking methods, and feature normalization techniques from the literature and define baseline requirements for single RGB camera tracking, plain paper homography, decoupled action profile mapping, and real-time CPU computation without specialized hardware.

\subsection{1.7.2 To Analyze...}
\label{subsec:obj_analyze}
To analyze hand joint kinematic features, unitless hand-length scale normalization, direct feature propagation, and temporal sliding windows (with 5 frames and 2-frame overlap at a 12~FPS standardized rate) to achieve camera resolution independence and real-time CPU computation without any need for a GPU.

\subsection{1.7.3 To Design and Develop...}
\label{subsec:obj_design}
To design, develop, and integrate an end-to-end two-application software suite comprising:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A PySide6 desktop layout designer (Application 1) for interactive key layout creation, generation of layout XML files, and printing PDF sheets with embedded AprilTag markers on standard plain paper.
    \item A runtime configuration and command mapper GUI (Application 2, Component A) through which users can choose regular RGB webcam feeds, load layout XML files, map buttons to keystrokes or shell commands, and save or switch between different action profiles for the same printed paper layout.
    \item An AprilTag visual tracking module to compute a real-time $3 \times 3$ Planar Homography matrix ($H$) from a single camera feed to correct perspective tilt and paper movement across regular and low camera resolutions \cite{Wang2016AprilTag2, Kallwies2020Determining}.
    \item A MediaPipe Hand Landmarker tracking module together with a hand-length scale normalizer that processes raw keypoints directly without temporal smoothing \cite{Zhang2020MediaPipe, SanchezBrizuela2023Lightweight}.
    \item A lightweight PyTorch LSTM touch detection model (under 1~MB file size) trained using 12~FPS 5-frame temporal windows for real-time touch detection of finger contact on standard CPUs without a GPU \cite{Hochreiter1997LSTM, GilMartin2023Hand, Simao2016Unsupervised}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{1.7.4 To Evaluate...}
\label{subsec:obj_evaluate}
To evaluate the complete system in terms of touch classification accuracy (precision, recall, F1-score), homography mapping precision, multi-profile action dispatch reliability on the same printed layout, CPU real-time latency across different camera resolutions (360p, 480p, 720p, 1080p), typing speed (Words Per Minute), Character Error Rate (CER), and overall usability on plain paper setups.

\subsection{Detailed Operational Architecture Steps}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Layout Design and Export:} The user designs a custom layout of keys using the PySide6 desktop designer software. This software generates two synchronized files: an XML file defining key region coordinates, button values, action commands, and anchor locations of AprilTag markers; and a printable PDF sheet displaying the layout grid with AprilTag markers placed along the borders.
    \item \textbf{Physical Paper Setup and Capture:} The user prints the PDF file on ordinary paper and places it on a flat table. An ordinary USB webcam or laptop camera captures the real-time video feed at any standard resolution.
    \item \textbf{AprilTag Tracking and Homography Calculation:} The AprilTag tracking module detects the four corner fiducial markers on the paper and determines their 2D pixel coordinates \cite{Wang2016AprilTag2, Kallwies2020Determining, Shabalina2018ARTag, Kalaitzakis2021Fiducial}. It computes a $3 \times 3$ Planar Homography matrix ($H$) to map camera pixel coordinates directly into the XML layout coordinate space \cite{Pirchheim2011Homography, Herdiansyah2024Implementation, GarridoJurado2026ArUco}.
    \item \textbf{Hand Tracking and Scale Normalization:} MediaPipe Hand Landmarker identifies 21 skeletal joint keypoints on detected hands \cite{Zhang2020MediaPipe, SanchezBrizuela2023Lightweight, Sreenath2021Monocular, Ivacko2024Edge, Andriyanov2025Improving}. These coordinates are normalized with respect to hand length ($L_{\text{hand}}$, the distance between the wrist and the middle MCP joint) to make features invariant to hand size, camera distance, and image resolution. Raw scale-normalized features are passed directly to the model without temporal smoothing to keep contact deceleration signals crisp.
    \item \textbf{Temporal Windowing:} Normalized joint positions and derived velocities are gathered at a 12~FPS rate into sliding temporal windows of 5 frames with a 2-frame overlap (a step size of 3 frames) \cite{Simao2016Unsupervised, Gammulle2021TMMF}.
    \item \textbf{LSTM Touch Classification on CPU:} The custom PyTorch LSTM neural network evaluates each 5-frame window on the CPU to detect whether a finger touched the physical surface \cite{Hochreiter1997LSTM, GilMartin2023Hand, Doan2022Efficient, Nunez2018Convolutional, Lai2018CNN}.
    \item \textbf{Homography Coordinate Mapping and Key Execution:} When a touch event is confirmed, the coordinates of the active fingertip pixel ($P_{\text{pixel}}$) are transformed using $H$ into the XML layout coordinate space ($P_{\text{XML}} = H \cdot P_{\text{pixel}}$) and matched against key boundaries to trigger the mapped keystroke or system shell command.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Resource Requirements}
\label{sec:resource_requirements}
The hardware and software specifications needed to build, run, and test the customizable paper-based virtual keyboard system are listed below.

\subsection{Hardware Requirements}
\label{subsec:hardware_req}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Monocular Camera:} Any ordinary USB webcam, low-end camera, or built-in laptop camera working at regular or low resolutions (such as 360p, 480p, 720p, or 1080p) is sufficient. High frame-rate or high-resolution cameras are not needed.
    \item \textbf{Host Computer:} A standard PC or laptop with an x86\_64 or ARM processor (such as an Intel Core i3/i5, AMD Ryzen, or basic equivalent, with at least 4~GB of RAM). The system runs in near real time on standard consumer CPUs without requiring high-end processors.
    \item \textbf{Graphics Card (Optional):} Live inference runs entirely on the CPU. A dedicated GPU is optional and not required for runtime execution.
    \item \textbf{Physical Materials:} A standard desktop printer with normal A4 or Letter paper, and a flat desk or table surface.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Software Requirements}
\label{subsec:software_req}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Operating System:} Linux (Ubuntu 22.04 LTS or 24.04 LTS), macOS, or Windows 11.
    \item \textbf{Programming Language and Tools:} Python 3.10 or higher, managed using the `uv` package manager.
    \item \textbf{GUI Framework:} PySide6 (Qt for Python) for desktop layout design and setup interfaces.
    \item \textbf{Computer Vision Libraries:} OpenCV (`opencv-python`) and Pupil AprilTag (`apriltag` Python bindings).
    \item \textbf{Hand Pose Estimation:} Google MediaPipe Hand Landmarker (`mediapipe`).
    \item \textbf{Deep Learning Framework:} PyTorch (`torch`, `torchvision`) for LSTM model definition, training, and real-time CPU inference.
    \item \textbf{Document Tools:} TeX Live full suite (`pdflatex`, `latexmk`, `biber`) for thesis compilation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{In-Scope Technical Boundaries}
\label{subsec:in_scope_boundaries}
When considering the hardware and sensing scope, the research strictly confines itself to the use of one simple monocular RGB webcam and ordinary plain printed sheets of paper \cite{Posner2012Single, Enkhbat2020HandKey, Sreenath2021Monocular}. The system is designed to function with low-cost USB webcams and laptop webcams at normal or low resolutions like 360p, 480p, 720p, and 1080p. No special high frame rate camera, studio optics, or depth sensors are required. The layout of the keyboard is printed on ordinary A4 or Letter paper via normal office desktop inkjet or laser printers. There are no electronics, wiring, or sensors embedded on the paper. Besides, the runtime algorithm is developed to run in real time on commodity consumer-grade CPUs without the need for a dedicated GPU or high-performance workstation.

In terms of interaction design and vision tracking, the design is aimed only at the active interaction of one hand and tracking of 21 landmarks of one active hand at a time. The deep learning model calculates the probabilities of touching of all five individual fingers of the active hand (Thumb, Index, Middle, Ring, and Pinky) simultaneously. Single-hand tracking helps to keep latency to a minimum ($29.09\text{ ms}$), and also to prevent hand occlusion of the four fiducial AprilTag border anchors on small A4 sheets of paper. Physical paper shape is separated from digital action semantics using PySide6 Layout Designer, enabling users to customize key layouts and assign the same physical printed sheet of paper to different action sets (typing, developer shortcuts, DAW music controls, or terminal commands) just by loading configuration profiles in the software \cite{Doan2022Efficient, Li2021CNN, Lee2022Virtual}. Vision tracking uses continuous AprilTag planar homography tracking that compensates for paper motion and camera angle \cite{Wang2016AprilTag2, Kalaitzakis2021Fiducial}. Hand landmarks are normalized to hand length without temporal smoothing delays at the standard 12~FPS rate in 5-frame sliding windows, and then classified using a compact PyTorch LSTM neural network on CPU \cite{Hochreiter1997LSTM, Zhang2020MediaPipe, GilMartin2023Hand}.

\subsection{Out-of-Scope Boundaries and Future Extensions}
\label{subsec:out_scope_boundaries}
In order to make the design feasible, low cost, and efficient on regular computer CPUs, a number of advanced features have been deliberately left out of the scope of this work. Firstly, with regard to the hardware aspect, specialized sensors for 3D depth detection, Time-of-Flight cameras, stereocameras, infrared laser pointers, and sensor gloves have been excluded \cite{Lee2019Virtual, Kudale2016RealTime}. Electronic paper and conductive ink circuitry are not included in the current design, nor is the typing on curved 3D surfaces, such as cylinders and spheres. Any input is only allowed on regular sheets of paper on flat surfaces. Secondly, with respect to interaction, two-handed typing and multi-user interaction have been ruled out of this project. Tracking of both hands at once would block the AprilTag marker detection on the usual A4 papers and overwhelm the CPU. Finally, complete 3D muscle force detection of fingers, calculations of touch pressures, and text autocompletion with a large language model are not part of this project and may be considered for further research.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:chapter_summary}
This introductory chapter has provided a detailed overview of the problem background, problem statement, research questions, motivation, aim, objectives, solution architecture, resource requirements, and project scope for a customizable paper-based virtual keyboard system.

Through the replacement of expensive specialized hardware (such as depth sensors, Time-of-Flight cameras, laser projectors, and high-end GPUs) with an ordinary RGB camera, standard plain printed paper, AprilTag planar homography tracking \cite{Wang2016AprilTag2, Kallwies2020Determining}, unitless scale normalization \cite{Zhang2020MediaPipe}, and a lightweight PyTorch LSTM touch classifier running in real time at 12~FPS on commodity CPUs \cite{Hochreiter1997LSTM, GilMartin2023Hand}, this research addresses key accessibility and technical challenges in monocular vision input interfaces. Chapter 2 (Literature Review) is the subsequent chapter that provides a comprehensive critical review of existing virtual keyboard frameworks, hand pose estimation algorithms, planar homography techniques, and deep learning gesture classification models in current literature.
