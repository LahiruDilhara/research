    {\fontsize{14pt}{18pt}\selectfont \textbf{ABSTRACT}\par}
    \vspace{1.5cm}
\end{center}

\noindent Physical computer keyboards are rigid, difficult to modify, and prone to pathogen transmission in public and clinical settings. Prior vision-based virtual keyboards attempt to solve these limitations, but they rely heavily on specialized 3D depth sensors, require high-end GPUs, or enforce artificial dwell-time pauses exceeding 500~ms to confirm key presses under standard 2D webcams. Furthermore, existing monocular systems are largely restricted to single-finger tracking, fail under variable room lighting and shadows, and depend on rigid camera mounts. This research develops and evaluates an accurate, real-time, and customizable paper-based virtual keyboard system using strictly a single regular monocular RGB webcam and commodity CPUs without specialized hardware. Physical keyboard layouts are created using an interactive PySide6 graphical designer and printed on normal paper with border AprilTag fiducial anchors, completely separating paper geometry from digital action semantics. The vision pipeline samples incoming video at 12~frames per second, extracts 21 skeletal hand landmarks via MediaPipe, and applies unitless hand-length scale normalization ($L_{\text{hand}}$). Kinematic temporal sliding windows of 5~frames with 2-frame overlap are classified across all five individual fingers simultaneously using a compact PyTorch Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) sequence model to detect surface contact deceleration instantaneously. Dynamic planar homography continuously updates coordinate transformations under camera perspective tilt up to $75^\circ$ and surface displacement. Comprehensive benchmarks confirm that the chosen LSTM model achieves a touch detection F1-score of 0.963 with 2.08~ms inference latency on CPU, maintaining an end-to-end processing latency of 29.09~ms (34.4~frames per second). Typing evaluations demonstrate an average typing speed of 24.6~words per minute with a 5.2\% character error rate. The framework delivers a low-cost, portable, and hygienic interface suitable for computing education, clinical environments, and flexible accessibility.
