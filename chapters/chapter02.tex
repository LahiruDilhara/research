\section{Chapter Overview}
\label{sec:lit_chapter_overview}
Due to constant innovation in the field of mobile computing technology, augmented and virtual reality, as well as computer systems being widely implemented into everyday objects, our approach towards interacting with computers has changed as well. For almost the entire period of the last century, the text input and generally computer interaction have been carried out using QWERTY mechanical keyboards and capacitive touch screens. Despite the fact that the mechanical keyboard allows the user to receive tangible tactile feedback and hence type quickly, the lack of mobility and fixation of the mechanical keyboard poses some challenges \cite{ReviewVirtualKeyboard2020, Lee2022Virtual}. First of all, the mechanical keyboard occupies the permanent space on the desk, is not flexible to changes of the software, can lead to infections in hospital wards or public kiosks, and is easily damaged due to liquid spills and dust accumulation \cite{ReviewVirtualKeyboard2020}.

In order to overcome this problem, a number of attempts have been made by the computer vision experts to create virtual input devices that can transform any two-dimensional plane into an input surface using the ordinary camera sensor \cite{Zhang2001Visual, Srivastava2012RealTime, Khare2019QWERTY, Maman2023TypeNet}. The virtual keyboard could be created using the method called monocular vision, which looks very promising because it uses a planar surface monitored only by one RGB camera without any additional switches or electric board components.

Nevertheless, the creation of an accurate, efficient, and adjustable virtual keyboard through a usual RGB video stream does not lack the challenges associated with computer vision, gesture recognition, and sequence learning. They are the following challenges:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Detection of Touch on Surface without Using Depth Sensors:} Monocular cameras with RGB color filters do not give any indication regarding depth along the Z-axis. The challenge arises in identifying the exact moment when the finger actually touches the surface in order to differentiate between pressing a button and touching the paper above the surface \cite{Thomas2013Camera, Posner2012Single, Lee2019Virtual, Toshpulatov2024RealTime}.
    \item \textbf{Hand Size and Signal Distortion:} In the hand landmark-based model, the output of the pixel coordinate is dependent on the size of the hand, the distance from the camera, and the position of the user \cite{Zhang2020MediaPipe, Doan2022Efficient}. Normalizing the spatial landmarks without applying temporal filtering, which would cause distortion of touch events, is crucial for detecting touch events \cite{Kumar2026Hybrid}.
    \item \textbf{Camera Tilt and Plane Orientation:} Even a slight misalignment between the paper and webcam alignment will lead to distortion of the perspective. This, in turn, makes the coordinate grids fail. It is therefore necessary that the planar markers and homography matrix ($H \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$) are computed in real time \cite{Wang2016AprilTag2, Kallwies2020Determining, Pirchheim2011Homography, Herdiansyah2024Implementation}.
    \item \textbf{Custom Design of Layouts and Mapping of Actions:} All virtual typing programs currently available use a standard QWERTY keyboard, but none of them allow for creating custom layouts in written form, allowing certain buttons to be assigned to particular keystrokes or shell commands \cite{ReviewVirtualKeyboard2020}.
\end{enumerate}

An in-depth critical review of the literature in vision-based virtual input, planar marker tracking, hand joint estimation, feature scaling, and deep learning sequence modeling will be discussed in this chapter. The way the literature has been organized is captured in the concept map provided in Section~\ref{sec:lit_conceptual_map}. In Section~\ref{sec:lit_domain_overview}, there is a brief overview of the field, which includes the history of text input, typing mechanism, ergonomics, and driving forces within the field. Section~\ref{sec:lit_existing_systems} provides the analysis of the available virtual keyboards using eight different kinds of sensors. The description of some basic algorithms and their processing technique is given in Section~\ref{sec:lit_technological_analysis}. The weaknesses of the existing approaches are highlighted in Section~\ref{sec:lit_reflection}.

\section{Domain Overview}
\label{sec:lit_domain_overview}
Entering text and executing commands are the primary ways humans communicate with computer systems. For more than a century, the mechanical QWERTY layout has been the standard layout for typing. Although mechanical keyboards provide helpful physical clicks that allow skilled users to type quickly, their rigid hardware design causes several problems in modern computing \cite{ReviewVirtualKeyboard2020}. Physical keyboards occupy fixed desk space, cannot change their button layouts for specific tasks (such as audio mixing, video editing, CAD design, or assistive typing), and can gather dust and bacteria in shared offices or sterile medical clinics.

\subsection{Historical Evolution of Text Entry and Spatial HCI Hardware}
\label{subsec:lit_history_evolution}
The development of text input interfaces can be divided into four main stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Mechanical and Electronic Keyboards (1870s to 1980s):} Starting with the first typewriters and moving to early computer keyboards like the IBM Model M, keys used physical levers and metal switches. The QWERTY layout was originally created to prevent mechanical typewriter bars from getting stuck together, rather than to maximize typing speed or comfort \cite{ReviewVirtualKeyboard2020}.
    \item \textbf{Membrane Keyboards and Touchscreens (1990s to 2000s):} Home computers popularized rubber dome membrane keyboards, while smartphones and tablets introduced capacitive touchscreens. Touchscreens allowed smooth multi-touch gestures but removed physical key edges and travel feedback \cite{ReviewVirtualKeyboard2020}.
    \item \textbf{Laser Projection and Infrared Curtains (2000s to 2010s):} To build portable keyboards for early mobile devices, engineers developed small laser projectors that projected red key outlines onto flat tables while scanning for fingers with infrared light curtains \cite{Cheng2015Fingertip, Kudale2016RealTime}. Although portable, these laser projectors drained batteries quickly and were hard to see in bright room lighting.
    \item \textbf{Monocular Vision, Hand Tracking, and Deep Learning (2010s to Present):} Recent deep learning models allow computers to track hand joints in real time using ordinary RGB webcams \cite{Zhang2020MediaPipe, GilMartin2023Hand, Maman2023TypeNet}. This approach makes it possible to create virtual keyboards on ordinary printed paper without needing active laser projectors or special sensors.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Kinematic Typing Dynamics and Biomechanical Surface Impact}
\label{subsec:lit_typing_biomechanics}
Understanding how human fingers move during typing is very important when designing vision-based touch detection models. When a user presses a key on a physical surface, the finger goes through four sequential movement stages \cite{Simao2016Unsupervised, MacRitchie2015Integrating, Pilkov2024Markerless}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Approach Stage ($t_1$ to $t_2$):} The finger speeds up as it moves downward from an idle resting height toward the paper. Downward velocity $V_y$ increases quickly.
    \item \textbf{Impact Stage ($t_2$ to $t_3$):} The fingertip hits the physical paper on the desk. In a very short time window ($\Delta t < 40$~ms), the downward speed drops to zero ($V_y \to 0$), producing a sharp deceleration change ($A_y = \frac{dV_y}{dt} \ll 0$).
    \item \textbf{Dwell Stage ($t_3$ to $t_4$):} The fingertip rests briefly on the surface while the computer registers the key press.
    \item \textbf{Recoil Stage ($t_4$ to $t_5$):} The finger lifts back up to an idle resting position, moving in the opposite direction ($V_y > 0$).
\end{enumerate}

When typing on a sheet of paper placed on a table, the flat table provides a natural physical stop during Stage 2, bringing the downward motion to a quick halt. In contrast, typing in mid-air lacks this physical resting surface, which causes fingers to overshoot and move inconsistently \cite{Boletsis2019TextInput}.

\subsection{Ergonomics, Fitts' Law, and Human Input Performance Metrics}
\label{subsec:lit_fitts_law}
Evaluating user typing performance across different input systems involves several standard metrics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Words Per Minute (WPM):} A standard speed metric calculated as $\text{WPM} = \frac{|T| - 1}{S} \times 60 \times \frac{1}{5}$, where $|T|$ is the number of typed characters and $S$ is the time taken in seconds.
    \item \textbf{Keystrokes Per Character (KSPC):} Measures typing accuracy by dividing the total number of keystrokes (including corrections) by the total character count. Physical keyboards usually achieve $\text{KSPC} \approx 1.05$, whereas mid-air typing systems often have $\text{KSPC} > 1.30$ because of unintended gestures.
    \item \textbf{Fitts' Law Model:} Predicts the movement time $MT$ needed to reach and press a key of width $W$ located at distance $D$:
    \begin{equation}
    MT = a + b \log_2 \left( 1 + \frac{D}{W} \right) = a + b \cdot ID
    \label{eq:fitts_law}
    \end{equation}
    where $ID$ is the Index of Difficulty in bits. Monocular paper keyboards let users enlarge key widths $W$ in the layout designer, lowering the difficulty value $ID$ for users who need larger buttons.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Physical Surface Interaction Paradigms: Desk vs. Paper vs. Floating Air}
\label{subsec:lit_surface_paradigms}
Comparing different physical interaction surfaces highlights several practical trade-offs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bare Desk Surface:} Provides a solid physical stop for finger presses, but lacks visible key outlines unless drawn by hand or projected \cite{Srivastava2012RealTime}.
    \item \textbf{Printed Paper Sheet:} Combines a solid physical stop with clear printed key labels and AprilTag tracking markers. Paper sheets do not consume electrical power, have zero display delay, and provide a persistent physical guide for the user \cite{Zhang2001Visual, Khare2019QWERTY}.
    \item \textbf{Floating Mid-Air Space:} Does not need any surface, but keeping arms raised in the air quickly causes arm fatigue ("Gorilla Arm Syndrome") and lacks physical touch feedback, leading to higher typing errors \cite{Boletsis2019TextInput, Enkhbat2020HandKey}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Biomechanical Kinematic Modeling of Multi-Finger Tendon Coupling}
\label{subsec:lit_biomechanical_coupling}
Along with single-finger movements, human hand anatomy involves natural physical connections between fingers \cite{Simao2016Unsupervised, Pilkov2024Markerless}. Tendons in the forearm and hand connect adjacent fingers, particularly between the middle finger (Landmarks 9 to 12) and the ring finger (Landmarks 13 to 16).

When a user presses a key with the index finger, neighboring fingers often move slightly as well. Simple algorithms that track only a single point (such as monitoring just the vertical height of an index finger) can mistakenly register these small sympathetic movements as secondary key clicks \cite{Thomas2013Camera, Srivastava2012RealTime}. Using deep learning models trained on complete 21-joint hand skeletons helps the software understand overall hand structure, allowing it to tell the difference between an intentional touch and passive movement from neighboring fingers.

\subsection{Domain Drivers for Customizable Paper Virtual Keyboards}
\label{subsec:lit_domain_drivers}
Monocular paper virtual keyboards provide practical benefits in several common situations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low-Cost Accessibility:} Using standard webcams and printable paper layouts removes the need for costly hardware, making virtual keypads practical for schools, home users, and developing areas \cite{Khare2019QWERTY, Sreenath2021Monocular}.
    \item \textbf{Custom Macro Keypads:} Programmers, sound designers, and graphic artists can design custom key sizes, specialized layouts, and macro pads tailored to specific software applications \cite{ReviewVirtualKeyboard2020}.
    \item \textbf{Clean Medical and Laboratory Settings:} In operating rooms, intensive care units, and cleanrooms, regular keyboards collect dust and bacteria. Inexpensive paper layouts can be laminated for easy cleaning or recycled after use \cite{ReviewVirtualKeyboard2020}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Existing Systems, Frameworks, and Sensing Modalities}
\label{sec:lit_existing_systems}
To understand the current state of virtual keyboards, prior systems are reviewed across eight different sensing approaches.

\subsection{Projection-Based and Infrared Sensor Systems}
\label{subsec:lit_proj_systems}
Projection-based virtual keyboards use small optical projectors combined with infrared (IR) light curtains or infrared cameras \cite{Kudale2016RealTime, Cheng2015Fingertip}. A typical laser keyboard projects a red keyboard pattern onto a flat table and projects an invisible infrared light layer a few millimeters above the surface. When a finger presses down, it breaks the infrared light layer, reflecting light back to an infrared sensor \cite{Cheng2015Fingertip}.

Kudale and Wanjale \cite{Kudale2016RealTime} built an infrared webcam tracking system using an IR-filtered camera and reflective pens, using 4-point planar homography to map projected keys to surface coordinates. Cheng et al. \cite{Cheng2015Fingertip} introduced an interactive projector-camera system that separated hands from the background using geometric calibration, estimating touch by tracking the distance between a fingertip and its shadow.

Although projection systems work on plain tables without paper, they face several hardware limitations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sensitivity to Ambient Light:} Bright sunlight or standard office lighting washes out laser patterns and interferes with infrared sensors, causing tracking failures.
    \item \textbf{Power Consumption and Cost:} Projection units need dedicated power sources and careful optical alignment, making them less practical for battery-powered setups.
    \item \textbf{Fixed Layouts:} The projected key pattern is fixed in the projector optics, so users cannot easily change key sizes or create custom button layouts.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Shadow-Based Monocular RGB Keyboards}
\label{subsec:lit_shadow_systems}
To avoid active laser projectors and extra infrared sensors, early monocular RGB virtual keyboard researchers studied shadow analysis to estimate finger touch from regular 2D video feeds \cite{Thomas2013Camera, Posner2012Single, Yue2014Blind}.

Thomas \cite{Thomas2013Camera} developed a single-camera virtual keyboard that tracked user fingertips and measured the pixel distance between a fingertip and its shadow. As a finger moves downward toward the desk, the distance between the fingertip and its shadow shrinks to zero at the moment of contact. Posner et al. \cite{Posner2012Single} extended this idea by adding adaptive thresholding to detect touch on paper layouts.

While shadow analysis allows touch detection on standard cameras without depth sensors, practical testing shows several serious weaknesses \cite{Yue2014Blind}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Need for Strong Directional Lighting:} Shadow tracking requires a single strong light source from a specific angle. Under diffuse ceiling lights or multiple fluorescent bulbs, shadows blur, split into multiple faint shapes, or disappear completely.
    \item \textbf{Hand Self-Occlusion:} The palm and other fingers frequently block light from reaching the table, hiding the shadows of the active typing fingers.
    \item \textbf{Background Surface Interference:} Dark, patterned, or reflective table surfaces make it hard for computer vision algorithms to separate shadows from the background, resulting in frequent missed or false key presses \cite{Yue2014Blind}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hardware-Assisted and Depth Sensor Systems (RGB-D / ToF)}
\label{subsec:lit_depth_systems}
With the release of consumer RGB-D depth cameras (such as Microsoft Kinect, Intel RealSense, and Time-of-Flight sensors), researchers built virtual touch systems that measure 3D coordinates directly \cite{Lee2019Virtual, Toshpulatov2024RealTime}.

Lee and Kwon \cite{Lee2019Virtual} introduced a virtual touch sensor using a ToF depth camera. By calibrating a reference depth plane for the table surface, the system detected touch events when fingertip point clouds came within a few millimeters of the surface. Toshpulatov et al. \cite{Toshpulatov2024RealTime} improved depth contact detection by combining 3D depth measurements with 2D optical flow motion vectors, helping filter out hovering fingers and false taps.

Depth cameras solve the shadow issues of regular RGB webcams by providing true 3D measurements. However, depth-based systems have practical drawbacks:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Higher Equipment Cost:} Depth cameras are significantly more expensive than regular webcams and are not built into budget laptops or desktop monitors.
    \item \textbf{Close-Range Noise and Minimum Distances:} Consumer depth cameras often have a minimum operating distance (around 20 to 40~cm) and produce noisy depth readings near flat table surfaces, making it hard to measure thin fingertips cleanly.
    \item \textbf{Higher Processing Load:} Processing detailed 3D point clouds in real time requires more computing power and often needs dedicated GPU hardware.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{3D Mid-Air Typing and AR/VR Floating Keyboards}
\label{subsec:lit_air_systems}
With the growth of virtual reality and augmented reality headsets (such as Meta Quest and Apple Vision Pro), researchers have explored mid-air typing interfaces where users type on virtual keys floating in space \cite{Boletsis2019TextInput, Enkhbat2020HandKey, Lee2022Virtual, Yoo2026WordLevel}.

Enkhbat et al. \cite{Enkhbat2020HandKey} introduced HandKey, a two-hand air-typing system using a single camera. HandKey tracks 3D finger joints in mid-air and uses Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to recognize key selections when fingertips enter pre-set 3D virtual boxes. Lee et al. \cite{Lee2022Virtual} designed two-handed virtual keyboards for headsets using deep learning keypoint detection, and Yoo and Lee \cite{Yoo2026WordLevel} proposed word-level motion learning to classify continuous mid-air typing trajectories.

While mid-air typing does not require a table or paper surface, usability studies highlight significant operational challenges:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Arm Fatigue:} Holding hands and arms unsupported in mid-air causes quick muscle fatigue ("Gorilla Arm Syndrome") during longer typing sessions \cite{Boletsis2019TextInput}.
    \item \textbf{Lack of Physical Stop:} When typing on paper or a desk, the finger stops instantly upon hitting the surface. In mid-air typing, the absence of a surface causes fingers to move past target keys, resulting in higher typing errors and slower typing speeds \cite{Boletsis2019TextInput, Maman2023TypeNet}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Paper-Anchored and Monocular RGB Keyboards}
\label{subsec:lit_paper_systems}
Paper-based virtual keyboards represent the specific area addressed by this research: using printed paper layouts with regular RGB webcams to enable typing on flat surfaces \cite{Zhang2001Visual, Srivastava2012RealTime, Khare2019QWERTY, Maman2023TypeNet}.

Early work by Zhang et al. \cite{Zhang2001Visual} introduced the "Visual Panel," which tracked paper sheet corners using monocular vision to calculate a homography matrix $H$ and map fingertip positions to paper coordinates. Srivastava and Tripathi \cite{Srivastava2012RealTime} built a paper keyboard using fingertip velocity analysis: the typing finger speeds up moving downward and slows down suddenly upon hitting the paper. Khare \cite{Khare2019QWERTY} used skin-color segmentation and fixed key region masks to detect key presses when fingertip positions paused over key areas.

Recently, Maman and Bermano \cite{Maman2023TypeNet} presented TypeNet, a deep learning framework that allows touch typing on uncalibrated flat surfaces using a single camera. TypeNet uses self-refinement and language model decoding to convert finger joint trajectories into text.

Even with these improvements, existing paper keyboard systems show several recurring weaknesses:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Unstable Markerless Tracking:} Early systems relied on manual corner selection or simple color thresholding \cite{Zhang2001Visual, Khare2019QWERTY}. When the paper shifts or lighting changes, coordinate tracking fails.
    \item \textbf{Fixed Pixel Thresholds:} Speed analysis methods \cite{Srivastava2012RealTime} rely on fixed pixel velocity thresholds. Because pixel movements change with camera distance and hand sizes, fixed limits do not work well across different users.
    \item \textbf{Rigid Layout Designs:} Systems like TypeNet \cite{Maman2023TypeNet} and HandKey \cite{Enkhbat2020HandKey} use fixed QWERTY layouts and do not provide tools for designing custom printable layouts with synchronized action configurations.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Video Keystroke Inference and Attack Side-Channels}
\label{subsec:lit_attack_systems}
An interesting related research area is video-based keystroke inference, where researchers study whether typing can be reconstructed from video recordings \cite{Yue2014Blind, Yang2022Towards}.

Yue et al. \cite{Yue2014Blind} showed that side-view video recordings of smartphone typing could reconstruct typed text. The method used optical flow to detect finger downward slowdown, calculated a homography matrix $H$ mapping phone screen edges to a keyboard model, and used shadow cues to confirm contact. Yang et al. \cite{Yang2022Towards} generalized video keystroke recovery to uncalibrated cameras, using self-supervised motion paths to extract typed characters directly from video frames without prior layout calibration.

Although these security studies focused on eavesdropping risks, the underlying computer vision techniques (such as planar homography calculations, motion deceleration tracking, and coordinate mapping) provide valuable ideas for building vision-based virtual keyboards.

\subsection{Specialized Optical, Acoustic, and Sensor-Fusion Modalities}
\label{subsec:lit_specialized_systems}
Beyond standard cameras, researchers have explored other specialized sensing techniques, including Visible Light Communication (VLC) shadow sensing \cite{Webber2021Finger}, fingernail blood volume deformation imaging \cite{Sun2009Estimation}, and synchronized motion capture for musical instruments \cite{MacRitchie2015Integrating, Pilkov2024Markerless}.

Webber et al. \cite{Webber2021Finger} used indoor ceiling light sensors and photodiode arrays to recognize finger gestures by measuring light changes caused by hand movement. Sun et al. \cite{Sun2009Estimation} developed a vision method to estimate fingertip pressing force by analyzing fingernail color changes under Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), showing that pressing a surface changes blood distribution under the nail. MacRitchie and McPherson \cite{MacRitchie2015Integrating} and Pilkov \cite{Pilkov2024Markerless} combined multi-camera motion capture with piano key touch sensors, proving that finger joint speeds show clear deceleration changes upon hitting a physical surface.

These studies confirm that hitting a physical surface produces clear motion changes, supporting the idea of detecting paper touch contact directly from scale-normalized hand joint movements.

\subsection{Gaze-Based and Assistive Visual Interfaces}
\label{subsec:lit_gaze_interfaces}
For users with severe motor limitations, eye-gaze tracking virtual keyboards offer an alternative touchless text entry option \cite{Chen2024Gaze}. Chen \cite{Chen2024Gaze} developed a gaze-based typing system using standard webcams without expensive eye-tracking hardware. The software tracked facial landmarks and pupil positions using deep neural networks to estimate gaze points on virtual keys, using dwell-time pauses to select letters.

Although gaze-based keyboards offer valuable accessibility options, dwell-time selection can cause accidental clicks when users pause naturally to read ("Midas Touch" problem) and is slower than multi-finger hand typing. Combining hand landmark tracking with paper surface contact provides a faster and more natural experience for users who can move their hands.

\subsection{Robotic Vision Servo Control and Fiducial Calibration Systems}
\label{subsec:lit_robotics_systems}
Visual fiducial markers are widely used in robotics for position estimation and camera calibration \cite{Ivacko2024Edge, Herdiansyah2024Implementation, Schenck2024Utilizing, Isaksson2020Camera, Oscadal2020Improved, GarridoJurado2026ArUco}.

Ivacko et al. \cite{Ivacko2024Edge} used wrist-mounted cameras with ArUco markers to compute coordinate transformations for robot arm gesture control. Herdiansyah et al. \cite{Herdiansyah2024Implementation} applied Zhang's camera calibration method to improve ArUco positioning accuracy, reaching 95\% localization precision. Schenck et al. \cite{Schenck2024Utilizing} created an image-editing pipeline that removed markers synthetically to train markerless keypoint detectors for robot control. Garrido-Jurado et al. \cite{GarridoJurado2026ArUco} introduced ArUco Nano, a fast marker detector achieving a 6.5 times speed increase using an optimized contour scanning algorithm.

These robotics studies show that fiducial markers provide stable and accurate planar alignment, supporting their use for paper keyboard homography calibration.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis of Machine Learning Models in Virtual Input}
\label{subsec:lit_ml_comparative}
Prior research has used various machine learning models to map visual features to typing actions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Heuristic Limits and Rule-Based Methods \cite{Srivastava2012RealTime, Posner2012Single}:} Require no training time but fail when user typing speed or lighting changes.
    \item \textbf{Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Random Forests \cite{Webber2021Finger}:} Fast to train on handcrafted spatial features, but lack sequence memory over time.
    \item \textbf{1D Convolutional Neural Networks (1D CNN) \cite{Lai2018CNN, Ikram2020Skeleton}:} Extract local motion patterns quickly across short sliding windows, but do not maintain recurrent hidden states.
    \item \textbf{Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) Networks \cite{Hochreiter1997LSTM, Doan2022Efficient, GilMartin2023Hand}:} Maintain internal memory cell states across sequential time steps, allowing the network to learn approach, impact, and recoil movement patterns.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Noise Modeling and Lighting Robustness in Passive Vision Systems}
\label{subsec:lit_noise_modeling}
Deploying monocular RGB computer vision systems in unconstrained indoor environments requires handling lighting changes and camera sensor noise:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Handling Different Light Levels:} Indoor light levels range from dim room light ($100$~lux) to bright office fluorescent lighting ($1,000$~lux) and strong sunlight ($50,000+$~lux). Older skin segmentation and shadow detection algorithms fail when lighting conditions change \cite{Yue2014Blind}.
    \item \textbf{Deep Landmark Detector Robustness:} Modern deep learning keypoint models (such as MediaPipe Hands \cite{Zhang2020MediaPipe}) are trained on diverse image datasets with data augmentation (varying contrast, brightness, and synthetic noise). Because of this training, predicted joint positions remain reliable across changing room lighting.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Hand Pose Estimation and Skeletal Joint Tracking}
Tracking 2D and 3D hand joint positions from video streams in real time without physical gloves or colored markers is central to vision-based interaction \cite{Cui2020Deep, Zhang2020MediaPipe}.

In early research, hand tracking relied on skin-color thresholding in color spaces like HSV or YCbCr \cite{Ji2018Local, Khare2019QWERTY}. However, skin-color segmentation is very sensitive to room lighting changes, cluttered backgrounds, and diverse skin tones. Modern deep learning models, such as OpenPose, YOLO-Pose \cite{Andriyanov2025Improving}, and Google MediaPipe Hand Landmarker \cite{Zhang2020MediaPipe}, improved hand tracking by predicting skeletal joint positions directly from image pixels.

Google MediaPipe Hands \cite{Zhang2020MediaPipe} uses a two-stage detection and tracking pipeline designed to run efficiently on standard CPUs:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Single-Shot Palm Detector:} The model first scans the full video frame to find palm bounding boxes. Instead of searching for moving fingers directly (which can block each other or bend in complex ways), the detector finds the palm and wrist. It uses focal loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{focal}} = -\alpha_t (1 - p_t)^\gamma \log(p_t)$ to find hands accurately across different sizes.
    \item \textbf{21-Landmark Keypoint Network:} Once the palm area is cropped, a landmark model predicts 2D coordinates $(x_i, y_i)$ and relative depth values $z_i$ for 21 anatomical hand joints, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:mediapipe_hand_topology}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Spatial Kinematic Scale Normalization vs. Temporal Smoothing}
Raw pixel coordinates $(x_i, y_i)$ produced by landmark detectors vary when the hand moves closer to or further from the camera, or when the camera resolution changes \cite{Doan2022Efficient, Kumar2026Hybrid}. If raw pixel values are sent directly into a neural network, a tap by a large hand near the camera appears as a much larger coordinate movement than the exact same tap performed by a smaller hand located further away.

\paragraph{Mathematical Proof of Perspective Scale Invariance:}
Under a standard pinhole camera projection model, 3D world coordinates $(X, Y, Z)$ map to 2D image pixel coordinates $(x_{\text{pixel}}, y_{\text{pixel}})$ using camera focal length $f$:
\begin{equation}
x_{\text{pixel}} = f \frac{X}{Z}, \quad y_{\text{pixel}} = f \frac{Y}{Z}
\label{eq:pinhole_projection}
\end{equation}

When the distance between the hand and the camera $Z$ changes by a factor $k$ ($Z' = k Z$), the resulting pixel coordinates scale inversely by $k$:
\begin{equation}
x'_{\text{pixel}} = \frac{x_{\text{pixel}}}{k}, \quad y'_{\text{pixel}} = \frac{y_{\text{pixel}}}{k}
\label{eq:pixel_scaling}
\end{equation}

In the same way, the measured hand length $L_{\text{hand}}$ on the image plane also scales by the factor $k$:
\begin{equation}
L'_{\text{hand}} = \frac{L_{\text{hand}}}{k}
\label{eq:hand_length_scaling}
\end{equation}

When landmark coordinates relative to the wrist are divided by $L_{\text{hand}}$, the distance factor $k$ cancels out:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}_i^{\text{norm}\prime} = \frac{\mathbf{P}_i' - \mathbf{P}_0'}{L'_{\text{hand}}} = \frac{\frac{\mathbf{P}_i - \mathbf{P}_0}{k}}{\frac{L_{\text{hand}}}{k}} = \frac{\mathbf{P}_i - \mathbf{P}_0}{L_{\text{hand}}} = \mathbf{P}_i^{\text{norm}}
\label{eq:scale_invariance_proof}
\end{equation}

Equation~(\ref{eq:scale_invariance_proof}) proves that unitless hand-length normalization makes feature values invariant to camera distance $Z$ and individual hand sizes.

Let $\mathbf{P}_0 = (x_0, y_0)$ represent the wrist (Landmark 0) and $\mathbf{P}_9 = (x_9, y_9)$ represent the middle MCP joint (Landmark 9). The hand length scale factor $L_{\text{hand}}$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
L_{\text{hand}} = \sqrt{(x_9 - x_0)^2 + (y_9 - y_0)^2}
\label{eq:hand_length_scale_def}
\end{equation}

Each landmark coordinate $\mathbf{P}_i = (x_i, y_i)$ is normalized relative to the wrist origin $\mathbf{P}_0$ and divided by $L_{\text{hand}}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}_i^{\text{norm}} = \left( \frac{x_i - x_0}{L_{\text{hand}}}, \frac{y_i - y_0}{L_{\text{hand}}} \right)
\label{eq:normalized_landmark_def}
\end{equation}

Numerical velocities $\mathbf{V}_i(t)$ between consecutive frames are calculated from these scale-normalized coordinates:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{V}_i(t) = \frac{\mathbf{P}_i^{\text{norm}}(t) - \mathbf{P}_i^{\text{norm}}(t - \Delta t)}{\Delta t}
\label{eq:joint_velocity_def}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Direct Raw Kinematic Feature Propagation and Information Value:}
In touchless interaction research (such as air-drawing or mid-air gesture recognition), low-pass smoothing filters or moving averages are often applied to remove small hand tremors \cite{Kumar2026Hybrid}.

However, for physical surface typing, landmark coordinates and calculated velocities must be sent directly to the model without temporal low-pass smoothing. The reasons for using direct feature propagation in touch detection are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Resolving the Spatial Ambiguity Bottleneck (Hover vs. Contact):} A stationary finger hovering $2\text{ mm}$ above a paper key produces virtually the same $(x, y)$ coordinates in a 2D camera image as a finger in physical contact. Pure spatial coordinates alone cannot distinguish hover from touch. Touch is fundamentally an impact dynamic event characterized by a rapid deceleration spike ($V_y \to 0$) upon desk impact \cite{Srivastava2012RealTime, MacRitchie2015Integrating}.
    \item \textbf{Preserving Touch Deceleration Signals:} Deliberate keystrokes involve sudden deceleration impacts that occur within a very short time window (under 40~ms). Low-pass smoothing filters smooth out these sharp velocity changes, weakening the key signal that separates a true surface tap from hovering finger motion.
    \item \textbf{Biomechanical Joint Tiers for Intent Discrimination:} Incorporating multiple joint tiers (terminal fingertips for contact impact, intermediate DIP/PIP knuckles for curvature and curl angles, and MCP/wrist base points for palm reference) allows the deep sequence model to separate intentional individual finger strikes from whole-hand posture shifts.
    \item \textbf{Avoiding Latency Delay:} Low-pass filters and moving averages introduce time delay across consecutive frames. In real-time typing, this introduces extra lag of several frames (30 to 90~ms), making the system feel less responsive to the user.
\end{itemize}

By using unitless hand-length scale normalization ($\mathbf{P}_i^{\text{norm}}$) with direct feature propagation, the pipeline preserves sharp velocity changes, allowing the recurrent neural network to detect surface contact quickly and accurately.

\subsubsection{Planar Homography Mathematics and AprilTag Fiducial Tracking}
Mapping 2D webcam pixel coordinates $(x_{\text{pixel}}, y_{\text{pixel}})$ to a printed paper layout $(x_{\text{XML}}, y_{\text{XML}})$ requires correcting for camera tilt, perspective distortion, and paper movement \cite{LonguetHiggins1985Machine, Pirchheim2011Homography, Wang2020Relative}.

Because both the physical paper sheet and the layout coordinate system are flat 2D planes, their geometric mapping is defined by a $3 \times 3$ Planar Homography Matrix $H \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ in projective geometry \cite{LonguetHiggins1985Machine, Pirchheim2011Homography}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
x_{\text{XML}} w \\
y_{\text{XML}} w \\
w
\end{bmatrix}
=
H \mathbf{P}_{\text{pixel}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
h_{11} & h_{12} & h_{13} \\
h_{21} & h_{22} & h_{23} \\
h_{31} & h_{32} & h_{33}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
x_{\text{pixel}} \\
y_{\text{pixel}} \\
1
\end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:homography_equation_def}
\end{equation}

Dividing by the scale factor $w = h_{31} x_{\text{pixel}} + h_{32} y_{\text{pixel}} + h_{33}$ gives the final XML coordinates:
\begin{equation}
x_{\text{XML}} = \frac{h_{11} x_{\text{pixel}} + h_{12} y_{\text{pixel}} + h_{13}}{h_{31} x_{\text{pixel}} + h_{32} y_{\text{pixel}} + h_{33}}, \quad
y_{\text{XML}} = \frac{h_{21} x_{\text{pixel}} + h_{22} y_{\text{pixel}} + h_{23}}{h_{31} x_{\text{pixel}} + h_{32} y_{\text{pixel}} + h_{33}}
\label{eq:homography_cartesian_def}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Direct Linear Transform (DLT) Matrix Formulation:}
Given $N \ge 4$ corresponding point pairs $(x_k, y_k) \leftrightarrow (x'_k, y'_k)$, each point pair produces two linear equations:
\begin{align}
-x_k h_{11} - y_k h_{12} - h_{13} + 0 + 0 + 0 + x_k x'_k h_{31} + y_k x'_k h_{32} + x'_k h_{33} &= 0 \label{eq:dlt_row1} \\
0 + 0 + 0 - x_k h_{21} - y_k h_{22} - h_{23} + x_k y'_k h_{31} + y_k y'_k h_{32} + y'_k h_{33} &= 0 \label{eq:dlt_row2}
\end{align}

Combining these equations yields the system $\mathbf{A}_{2N \times 9} \mathbf{h} = \mathbf{0}$. Solving this system using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD, where $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{U} \mathbf{\Sigma} \mathbf{V}^T$) gives vector $\mathbf{h}$ from the column in $\mathbf{V}$ associated with the smallest singular value.

\paragraph{Comparative Analysis of Visual Fiducial Marker Systems:}
To provide reliable reference points for homography calculation, visual fiducial markers are placed at the corners of the printed layout \cite{Fiala2010Designing, Wang2016AprilTag2, Kallwies2020Determining, Shabalina2018ARTag, Kalaitzakis2021Fiducial, GarridoJurado2026ArUco, Zhang2022DeepTag}. The field of planar fiducial tracking has evolved across three key generations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Foundational Edge-Based Digital Markers (ARTag):} Fiala \cite{Fiala2010Designing} introduced the seminal ARTag architecture, establishing a two-stage pipeline consisting of edge-gradient based candidate quad hypothesis generation and forward-error-correcting digital verification. This eliminated the lighting sensitivity and high false-positive rates of earlier correlation-based templates.
    \item \textbf{Sub-Pixel Line Clustering and Hamming Code Systems (AprilTag and ArUco):} AprilTag 2/3 \cite{Wang2016AprilTag2} and ArUco \cite{GarridoJurado2026ArUco} improved corner localization through sub-pixel gradient line clustering and adaptive thresholding. Comparative studies by Shabalina et al. \cite{Shabalina2018ARTag}, Kallwies et al. \cite{Kallwies2020Determining}, and Kalaitzakis et al. \cite{Kalaitzakis2021Fiducial} showed that AprilTag achieves a low median corner error of 0.017 pixels even at camera tilt angles up to $75^\circ$, outperforming ArUco under steep viewing angles and low image resolutions.
    \item \textbf{Deep Learning Keypoint Marker Detectors (DeepTag):} Zhang et al. \cite{Zhang2022DeepTag} developed DeepTag, an end-to-end CNN framework that regresses dense internal keypoints (such as all internal grid cell centroids) and decodes digital IDs under severe motion blur and extreme viewing angles.
\end{enumerate}

While deep learning marker frameworks like DeepTag \cite{Zhang2022DeepTag} offer high robustness, traditional AprilTag detection \cite{Wang2016AprilTag2} runs in under $1.0$~ms on commodity CPUs with sub-pixel precision. This leaves computational headroom on standard CPUs for concurrent MediaPipe joint extraction and PyTorch touch classification without requiring a GPU. Therefore, border AprilTag markers were chosen for our real-time paper keyboard system.

\subsubsection{Deep Sequential Temporal Touch Detection Models}
Detecting finger surface touches from continuous normalized landmark sequences requires analyzing motion patterns across multiple frames over time \cite{Hochreiter1997LSTM, Nunez2018Convolutional, Gammulle2021TMMF}.

Simple limit-based methods (such as triggering a key whenever downward velocity $V_y > \theta$) fail because non-touch hand adjustments, hovering movements, and natural finger coupling create velocity variations that trigger false presses \cite{Simao2016Unsupervised, Toshpulatov2024RealTime}.

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), especially Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks \cite{Hochreiter1997LSTM}, are well-suited for classifying time-series motion data \cite{Doan2022Efficient, GilMartin2023Hand, Nunez2018Convolutional, Ikram2020Skeleton}. The standard LSTM architecture uses internal memory cells and gating equations to learn sequential patterns:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{f}_t &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_f [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_f) \quad &&\text{(Forget Gate)} \label{eq:lstm_forget_def} \\
\mathbf{i}_t &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_i [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_i) \quad &&\text{(Input Gate)} \label{eq:lstm_input_def} \\
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}_t &= \tanh(\mathbf{W}_c [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_c) \quad &&\text{(Candidate State)} \label{eq:lstm_candidate_def} \\
\mathbf{C}_t &= \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{C}}_t \quad &&\text{(Cell State Update)} \label{eq:lstm_cell_def} \\
\mathbf{o}_t &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_o [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_o) \quad &&\text{(Output Gate)} \label{eq:lstm_output_def} \\
\mathbf{h}_t &= \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tanh(\mathbf{C}_t) \quad &&\text{(Hidden Output)} \label{eq:lstm_hidden_def}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Comparative Deep Neural Architecture Evaluation:}
When modeling 1D time-series skeletal hand sequences, researchers evaluate five primary deep learning architectures \cite{Lai2018CNN, Ikram2020Skeleton, Nunez2018Convolutional, Doan2022Efficient, Gammulle2021TMMF}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Uni-directional LSTM Networks:} Process sequence $\mathbf{X} = (\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_T)$ forward in time. Uni-directional LSTMs rely only on current and past frames, making them ideal for live real-time streams where future frames are not yet available.
    \item \textbf{Bidirectional LSTMs (BiLSTM):} Combine a forward hidden state $\overrightarrow{\mathbf{h}}_t$ with a backward hidden state $\overleftarrow{\mathbf{h}}_t$. While BiLSTMs achieve high accuracy on pre-recorded datasets, the backward pass requires waiting for future frames, which introduces unwanted buffering latency in live typing.
    \item \textbf{1D Convolutional Neural Networks (1D CNN):} Apply 1D temporal convolution filters across landmark features. 1D CNNs extract local patterns quickly, but do not maintain recurrent memory states over longer motions.
    \item \textbf{1D Residual Networks (1D ResNet):} Use skip-connections $\mathbf{y} = \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{x}$ to help train deeper temporal convolutional layers while avoiding vanishing gradients.
    \item \textbf{Self-Attention and Transformer Networks (Attention):} Use multi-head self-attention mechanisms $\text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) \mathbf{V}$ to capture pairwise dependencies across all temporal frames in a sequence.
\end{enumerate}

Our experimental benchmark using \texttt{run\_all.py} across 22 model combinations confirmed that a uni-directional PyTorch LSTM model trained on 5-frame scale-normalized sequences provides the best balance of high classification accuracy and low real-time CPU latency.

\paragraph{Temporal Windowing Architecture:}
To evaluate incoming landmark data, input features are grouped into sliding temporal windows \cite{Simao2016Unsupervised, Doan2022Efficient, Gammulle2021TMMF}. The sliding window configuration balances detection accuracy with processing speed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{5-Frame Window with 2-Frame Overlap (Stride of 3 Frames):} When running at a standardized 12~FPS video rate, a 5-frame temporal window covers a duration of $T = 416.6$~ms. A 2-frame overlap (step size of 3 frames) means the PyTorch LSTM evaluates touches every 250~ms. Experimental testing shows that 5 frames provide enough temporal context to capture finger descent, surface impact, and finger recoil while keeping processing latency low.
\end{itemize}

Model benchmarks across 2D architectures (LSTM, BiLSTM, 1D CNN, 1D ResNet, Self-Attention) confirm that a multi-layer PyTorch LSTM model trained on 5-frame scale-normalized sequences achieves strong classification performance for multi-finger touch detection on standard computer processors.

\subsubsection{Layout Customization and Synchronized XML Artifacts}
A key limitation found in earlier research \cite{ReviewVirtualKeyboard2020, Maman2023TypeNet} is that layout design tools are rarely integrated with runtime typing engines. Most prior virtual keyboards hardcode fixed QWERTY key coordinates directly into the source code.

To make the system flexible, the architecture separates layout creation into a dedicated Layout Designer application (\texttt{designer/designer\_app.py}). This tool lets users draw custom button shapes, assign keys to specific keystrokes or shell commands, and automatically place AprilTag fiducial markers at layout corners. When the user finishes a design, the tool exports two synchronized files:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Printable Layout PDF:} A PDF file showing the key boundaries and embedded AprilTag visual markers, ready for printing on standard paper.
    \item \textbf{Layout XML Specification:} A structured XML file containing the coordinates of every key $(x_{\min}, y_{\min}, x_{\max}, y_{\max})$, key identifiers, labels, command actions, and marker anchor locations.
\end{enumerate}

During live operation, the runtime software reads this XML file to set up key lookup boundaries and homography target spaces automatically, without needing to recompile the program.

\section{Reflection and Synthesis of Research Gap}
\label{sec:lit_reflection}
A careful review of prior work shows important achievements along with several remaining limitations in vision-based human-computer interaction.

\subsection{Synthesis of Prior Literature Limitations}
The review of existing systems reveals six major limitations and research gaps in previous research:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Inability to Track Multiple Fingers Independently:} Traditional monocular camera keyboards are restricted to tracking only a single active finger (usually the index finger or the single fastest-moving fingertip) \cite{Thomas2013Camera, Ji2018Local, Srivastava2012RealTime}. In shadow-based systems, when multiple fingers move near the surface together, their cast shadows merge and occlude one another \cite{Posner2012Single}, causing the touch detector to fail. Consequently, prior systems cannot recognize natural touch events across all five individual fingers of the hand.
    \item \textbf{Reliance on Artificial Dwell-Time Delays:} Because standard 2D webcams cannot measure physical contact directly, earlier vision keyboards forced the user to hover and freeze their finger over a key for a fixed duration (dwell-time of 500~ms to 1000~ms) to register a press \cite{Khare2019QWERTY, Chen2024Gaze}. This artificial delay introduces high input latency, causes typing fatigue, and prevents natural, continuous typing.
    \item \textbf{High Computing Overhead and GPU Requirements on Commodity Hardware:} Existing deep learning vision models that process high-resolution frames or complex 3D grids drop below 15~FPS on standard CPUs \cite{Enkhbat2020HandKey, Ji2018Local}, while other systems require dedicated GPUs or high-end workstations to achieve real-time responsiveness.
    \item \textbf{Environmental Brittleness Under Varying Lighting and Shadows:} Shadow-based and color-thresholding methods break down under diffuse room lighting, multiple light bulbs, soft hand shadows, and diverse skin tones or cluttered backgrounds \cite{Thomas2013Camera, Yue2014Blind, ReviewVirtualKeyboard2020}.
    \item \textbf{Reliance on Expensive or Specialized Hardware:} To avoid monocular depth ambiguity, many systems rely on specialized hardware such as Time-of-Flight depth cameras, stereo camera rigs, laser projectors, or wearable sensor gloves \cite{Lee2019Virtual, Kudale2016RealTime, Toshpulatov2024RealTime, Pilkov2024Markerless}. This reliance makes systems expensive and prevents them from running on standard computers with only a regular webcam.
    \item \textbf{Lack of Integrated Layout Customization, Marker Tracking, and Action Multiplexing:} Existing deep learning typing systems (such as TypeNet \cite{Maman2023TypeNet} and HandKey \cite{Enkhbat2020HandKey}) use fixed QWERTY layouts and lack design tools to create printable paper sheets with tracking markers. Without continuous homography tracking ($H$), any slight shift of the paper breaks coordinate accuracy \cite{Wang2016AprilTag2, Kallwies2020Determining, Pirchheim2011Homography}. Furthermore, prior systems cannot dynamically multiplex the same physical printed sheet across multiple software profiles without reprinting.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Formal Rationale and Research Gap Justification}
These limitations clearly define the central research gap addressed by this thesis:

\begin{quote}
\emph{There is an absence of an integrated, real-time virtual keyboard architecture that operates using strictly a single regular monocular RGB camera on standard plain printed paper layouts without specialized hardware (no depth sensors, no lasers, no stereo pairs), detects independent multi-finger touch events without artificial dwell-time delays, executes in real time entirely on standard commodity CPUs without requiring a dedicated GPU (while remaining GPU-accelerable), supports dynamic layout customization and action multiplexing on the same printed sheet, and maintains robust touch detection and homography mapping across regular and low camera resolutions at a 12~FPS operational standard.}
\end{quote}

By combining custom paper layout design, AprilTag homography tracking, unitless hand-length scale normalization, direct feature propagation, and a lightweight PyTorch LSTM touch classifier, the system built in this research overcomes the lighting issues, hardware costs, single-finger restrictions, dwell-time latency, high computing demands, and rigid layouts found in earlier systems.
