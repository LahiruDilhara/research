\section{Overview of the Chapter and Main Takeaways}
\label{sec:concl_overview}
In this chapter, we summarize the findings, practical challenges, and main takeaways from developing our paper-based virtual macro keyboard. At the beginning of this project, the driving question was whether an ordinary webcam pointed at plain paper could serve as a fast and reliable macro input device on a standard CPU without needing specialized depth sensors or laser projectors. This chapter analyzes whether the planned objectives were accomplished by comparing them against our empirical test results. I also examine the technical problems and solutions encountered during development, personal reflections and skills learned, potential commercial applications, and realistic directions for future improvement.

\section{Verification and Triangulation of Project Objectives}
\label{sec:concl_objectives}
To assess whether the research succeeded, I cross-referenced the empirical test outcomes with the original expectations set at the beginning.

\subsection{Addressing core literature gaps and initial baseline needs}
\label{subsec:concl_obj1}
The first objective was to identify what happens when past paper and virtual keyboards were delivered in real-world implementations. Through a review of eight different sensing modalities in literature (Figure~\ref{fig:lit_conceptual_map_diagram}, Table~\ref{tab:lit_comparative_matrix}), I was able to identify six primary limitations most existing monocular systems suffer from, such as limits in single-finger movement, discomfort due to dwell-time delays, and reliance on costly depth cameras. These concrete literature gaps helped to ground what this project needed to achieve in order to be more than just a theoretical concept.

\subsection{Validating hand kinematics and unitless scale invariance}
\label{subsec:concl_obj2}
The second goal involved distinguishing between key press activities and general movements of hands without any lagging. Normalization of hand coordinates through the unitless hand-length ratio ($L_{\text{hand}} = \|\mathbf{P}_9 - \mathbf{P}_0\|_2$) ensured independence of skeletal points from the distance of hands from the camera. Transmission of 5-frame sliding windows directly to the neural network at a rate of $12\text{ FPS}$ allowed capturing deceleration ($V_y \to 0$) on surface contact in real time without smoothing delays. Touch detection accuracy was validated in experimental settings where F1-scores remained greater than $0.958$ for distances ranging from $20\text{ cm}$ to $40\text{ cm}$.

\subsection{Performance outcomes for deep learning touch detection}
\label{subsec:concl_obj3}
The third target was to find a sequence model that is able to classify all five fingers simultaneously while running in real-time on a standard CPU. Out of 22 deep learning architectures assessed in our benchmarking suite (\texttt{modelBenchmark/benchmark\_engine.py}), the uni-directional PyTorch LSTM ranked first. It achieved an F1-score of $0.963$ with a per-finger average F1 of $0.965$ while only needing an average of $2.08\text{ ms}$ of inference time per window and taking up a compact size of only $0.44\text{ MB}$ in memory. This demonstrated that multi-finger touch detection can be implemented efficiently on consumer computers with no GPU.

\subsection{Evaluating the two-application software suite and custom layouts}
\label{subsec:concl_obj4}
Fourth, a full-fledged software suite involving two applications was developed to enable users to design customized layouts and connect any actions. Application~1, referred to as Layout Designer, enables users to design visual keyboard layouts with border AprilTags, saving them to XML and printable PDF formats. Application~2, known as Runtime Engine, loads the XML file, calculates planar homography in real time ($P_{\text{XML}} = H \cdot P_{\text{pixel}}$), and executes the pre-defined keystrokes. During functional testing, all ten test cases, namely FT-01 through FT-10, were executed successfully with a $100\%$ success rate, confirming that custom layout creation and action multiplexing operate reliably on a single printed sheet.

\subsection{End-to-end latency benchmarks and macro dispatch reliability}
\label{subsec:concl_obj5}
Fifth, to measure end-to-end latency and reliability of the system in real-world use. The end-to-end latency across full pipeline tests (webcam frame capture through to keystroke output) was $29.09\text{ ms}$ ($34.4\text{ FPS}$), well within our $83.33\text{ ms}$ budget at a rate of $12\text{ FPS}$. In fact, the background system itself used $142.5\text{ MB}$ of RAM and about a quarter of a single core ($24.8\%$) of CPU time. The homography error remained below $0.42\text{ mm}$ at perspective tilt angles up to $60^\circ$ using AprilTag tracking, and the LSTM model achieved $94.34\%$ accuracy and a $94.37\%$ F1-score on held-out test windows, highlighting how responsive the system is on common consumer hardware.

\section{Practical Problems Encountered and Technical Solutions}
\label{sec:concl_problems}
A number of practical problems were faced during system testing, but were overcome by making precise technical modifications.

\subsection{Webcam frame rate instability and the 13~FPS hardware finding}
\label{subsec:concl_prob_fps}
When the system was tested using a standard webcam, there was visible tracking jitter despite the fact that the driver promised $30\text{ FPS}$. Using a Python program to test the timestamps revealed that the actual rate of the camera was only $13\text{ FPS}$ in usual lighting conditions. Recurrent neural networks operate on velocities in discrete time steps ($\Delta t$), and the variations in frame intervals resulted in inaccurate motion features. The pipeline was fixed to run at a consistent sub-sampling rate of $12\text{ FPS}$, and a budget of $83.33\text{ ms}$ was allotted which comfortably accommodates our $29.09\text{ ms}$ CPU latency.

\subsection{Transition from freehand drawn paper to digital layout generation}
\label{subsec:concl_prob_freehand}
The first approach consisted of letting users draw key boxes on paper with a pen and using OpenCV contour detection to get the location of keys. Practically, this collapsed with webcam video as jagged lines, ink color variations, and faint shadows caused keys to fuse or vanish. Additionally, users were required to execute an inconvenient camera initialization scan with an empty sheet of paper prior to any typing. This was totally fixed by switching to Application~1 (Layout Designer). It stores a complete digital layout in XML, and the software explicitly knows key boundaries so there is no initialization step, allowing users to start typing immediately.

\subsection{Solving corner marker occlusion through coordinate projection}
\label{subsec:concl_prob_markers}
In the initial designs, there were four markers placed at the corners of the paper. While typing, user hands would often cover the markers below, which interfered with homography computation and caused the keyboard coordinates to freeze. In order to address this issue, I made use of two strategies. First, since the keyboard layout is already stored in XML, the camera only has to detect the paper plane. Second, I removed the four isolated corner markers and substituted them with redundant AprilTags along the margins of the paper. This made it possible for the hand to obscure one or two of the tags without preventing enough visible tags from existing to estimate the planar homography matrix $H$ reliably.

\subsection{Eliminating hand transit false triggers and breaking the 82\% accuracy plateau}
\label{subsec:concl_prob_dataset}
Previously, in early model iterations, the dataset simply logged 2D fingertip coordinates, limiting test accuracy to just $82\%$ as hand movements across keystrokes were often misidentified as intentional taps. I then built a custom annotation tool and re-annotated $3,800\text{ seconds}$ of typing footage using all 21 MediaPipe skeletal landmarks. Adding the wrist joint as a static anchor point to separate overall hand transit from purposeful finger taps improved test accuracy to $94.34\%$.

\subsection{Addressing anatomical finger coupling and providing interaction feedback}
\label{subsec:concl_prob_feedback}
Due to the physical connection between the flexor muscles of a person's fingers, when one presses their finger, this automatically causes downward dips in adjacent fingers as well. Moreover, flat paper has no tactile switch movement. In order to stop accidental triggering due to sympathetic movements, the training of the PyTorch LSTM was done using multi-label binary cross-entropy loss ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}$) for all five fingers together. Typing feedback is provided by Application~2 (Runtime Engine) by using audio clicks with latency less than $5\text{ ms}$ and marking the key as green on the live overlay display when pressed.

\section{Self-Reflection}
\label{sec:concl_reflection}
This research not only provided meaningful technical growth for me, but also taught me software engineering lessons in practice.

\subsection{Evolution of my ideology during the project}
\label{subsec:concl_ideology}
With this project, I was excited to make a virtual keyboard that is more accessible. In the beginning, my ideas were naive. I envisioned users drawing keys on a piece of paper with a pen, showing that to the camera for an initial scan, and typing without needing any special fiducial markers.

When I began coding and testing, I realized that hand-drawn lines on paper led to severe detection errors caused by lighting changes and camera blurring. The big discovery was when I split the process of creating a layout from camera tracking. By designing layouts using digital tools in Application~1 (Layout Designer) and saving key coordinates to an XML file, the program already knows the coordinates of each key. This saved users from an inconvenient scanning step, allowing them to start typing immediately. The task of the webcam is just to compute the planar homography matrix $H$.

Going through this changed my perception of human-computer interaction and computer vision. Initially, I thought that responsive touch systems were only feasible with expensive hardware such as depth sensors or dedicated graphics processing units (GPUs). With this project, I demonstrated that thoughtful software architecture, projective mathematics, and a lightweight PyTorch neural network can give timely touch functionality on a normal commodity CPU at virtually zero cost.

\subsection{Core technical skills and benefits gained}
\label{subsec:concl_benefits}
Through this research, I have gained solid technical competencies in a number of computer science domains. In computer vision, I was able to calibrate webcams, find the intrinsic matrix $K$, undistort lenses, detect AprilTags, and compute the planar homography matrix $H$ with Singular Value Decomposition (SVD). In deep learning, I created kinematic motion models with unitless scale normalization ($L_{\text{hand}}$), implemented LSTM networks with PyTorch, and benchmarked these models with automatic benchmark scripts (\texttt{run\_all.py}). In software engineering, I wrote full-fledged desktop applications in PySide6 with multi-threading using a producer-consumer pattern with \texttt{QThread} and \texttt{QMutex} to ensure that video capturing and model inference occur independently. This project also taught me the basics of rigorous academic research through quantitative benchmarking and technical documentation in LaTeX per IEEE standards.

\subsection{Key learning curves and practical takeaways}
\label{subsec:concl_learning_curves}
This whole process taught me plenty of engineering lessons along the way. The first thing I learned was not to take claims about hardware on faith: while the driver for my webcam reported it could do $30\text{ FPS}$, logging timestamps directly showed that it actually only ran at $13\text{ FPS}$. To ensure consistent temporal inputs across machines, the pipeline was locked into a steady $12\text{ FPS}$ sub-sampling rate. Second, five neural networks were costly to run individually, but combining all 21 landmarks into a single PyTorch LSTM classified five fingers at the same time in only $2.08\text{ ms}$, saving significant CPU time. Third, an investigation into why accuracy plateaued at $82\%$ found that false touches were created by whole-hand transit; re-annotating $3,800\text{ seconds}$ of typing footage using the wrist as a stationary anchor solved this problem, raising test accuracy to $94.34\%$. Fourth, we did not want touch decelerations to be split across window edges, so we added a 2-frame overlap between 5-frame sliding windows. Lastly, having redundant AprilTags along page margins eliminated corner occlusion failures, while adding ruler calibration addressed physical printer scaling differences.

\section{Business Insight of the Proposed Concept}
\label{sec:concl_business}
Initially, I did not have much concern for commercial feasibility when beginning this project since I needed to make touch detection feasible on monocular video. Comparing the results of our method with that of commercial macro equipment gives us an advantage from a commercial standpoint. Hardware macro equipment such as Elgato Stream Deck is priced at anywhere from \$50 to \$250. On the other hand, our method requires merely a printed paper sheet which costs less than \$0.02 and a standard webcam. This method is run in real-time using simple CPUs with no need for GPUs.

\subsection{What it could cost compared to existing products}
\label{subsec:concl_commercial}
By employing action profile multiplexing, we can merely print a single paper layout but have that serve completely different purposes such as video editing, audio mixing, developer shortcuts, or gaming without re-printing any of the paper. One could argue this removes the requirement for many specialist hardware macro pads. Dedicated macro hardware usually costs from \$50 to \$250 depending on the manufacturer. And we have managed to substitute all that by one piece of paper priced below \$0.02 and an average webcam.

\subsection{Where this could work in the real world}
\label{subsec:concl_applications}
In creative media production, hardware macro decks such as Elgato Stream Deck have prices higher than \$300, which makes it unaffordable for many students or amateur video editors. Using our system, a user just prints out an A4 piece of paper with large custom keys that allow one to cut, do color grading, or set audio balance. The printed piece of paper is left on the table, and Application~2 changes configurations between profiles of Premiere Pro, Blender, and Ableton Live depending on a context of work. For system administrators and software developers, common terminal actions such as \texttt{docker-compose up -d}, git commits, or server restarts can be made quickly and easily without manual repetitive typing.

In hospitals and clinics, regular computer keyboards attract microbes and become dysfunctional when exposed to disinfectants. The use of a paper keyboard means that it is cheaper at only \$0.02 per changeover of patient, avoiding cross-contamination risks entirely. In chemical industries and cleanrooms, regular mechanical keyboards could start sparking, hence the need for expensive explosion-proof certification. There is nothing about the paper keyboard that could spark, as there are no electrical components or wires on it, while the webcam monitors typing safely from behind a transparent barrier. With regard to schools that have limited funds to buy new computer equipment, teachers can easily print interaction papers and visual programming schemes for inexpensive laptops. In addition, with respect to users who have challenges such as motor impairments and hand tremors in using conventional compact keyboards, Application~1 allows caregivers to develop custom keyboard schemes with big target keys tailored to their physical reaching capacity.

\section{Future Technical Improvements and Research Directions}
\label{sec:concl_future}
While our results through the experiments demonstrate the reliability of the system, there are still many useful paths of development in the future. Firstly, obtaining the training data from additional views of the side cameras would allow the user to place a webcam anywhere on the table without decreasing the accuracy of tracking. Secondly, training the neural network in the future to identify the exact timestamp of the touch inside a 5-frame window would make the post-processing easier. Thirdly, using cameras with a high frame rate and less visual noise would decrease motion blur, providing clearer MediaPipe landmark detections. Fourthly, small depth estimation models such as MiDaS-nano can give rough estimates of surface distances to help with hand kinematics. Fifthly, scaling up the solution for two-handed bimanual typing is possible within our processing budget of $12\text{ FPS}$ where about $33\text{ ms}$ of CPU time is needed for each $83.33\text{ ms}$ window. Sixth, using deep learning fiducial detectors such as DeepTag \cite{Zhang2022DeepTag} will improve the corner detection under conditions of heavy camera lens blur. Furthermore, analysis of lightweight temporal Transformers could help to learn longer motion sequences while reducing accidental trigger rates.


\section{Summary of the Study and Concluding Remarks}
\label{sec:concl_summary}
This final chapter has brought together everything from the whole research process, connecting the triangulation of project objectives and engineering solutions to personal reflections on lessons learned, business perspectives, and realistic research recommendations for the future. All in all, this research has shown that it is possible to develop a customizable paper virtual macro keyboard, which uses a standard monocular webcam, AprilTag planar homography ($H$), unitless hand normalization ($L_{\text{hand}}$), and a lightweight PyTorch LSTM network in order to provide real-time touch detection ($29.09\text{ ms}$ latency, $34.4\text{ FPS}$, $94.34\%$ accuracy, $94.37\%$ F1-score) on commodity CPUs without the need for dedicated GPUs or specialized hardware. The solution is inexpensive, hygienic, and versatile due to the replacement of costly physical equipment with projective geometry and efficient deep learning.
